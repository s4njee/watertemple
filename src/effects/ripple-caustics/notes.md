## How it works

Caustics are where a wavy surface focuses sunlight. This demo does not draw a pattern and animate it: it takes the live heightfield of the ripple solver (`src/gpu/sim/heightfield.ts`, the surface you stir) and works out where each beam of sunlight ends up on the floor, so a drop sends a ring of light sweeping across the tiles, offset from the wave by the slant of the sun and the depth of the water. The code is `src/gpu/sim/caustics.ts` (`createHeightfieldCaustics`) with `caustics-core.wgsl`, `caustics-mesh.wgsl` and `caustics-jacobian.wgsl`; this folder is only the demo around it.

Frame per frame: the solver steps (splat, N steps, resolve), then **one caustics pass** reads its output texture (height, velocity, slope x, slope y) and writes an `rgba16float` texture over the same domain, indexed by FLOOR position, with intensity 1 meaning "the light a flat surface would give". Then the shading pass refracts the tiled floor through the surface (one tap of the same texture), looks up the caustics where the refracted ray hits the floor, so the light stays glued to the tiles however the water moves, and absorbs the result by depth. The floor tiles are drawn once into a texture, like the photograph a real site would use.

**What the caustics pass computes.** A sunbeam (direction `lightDir`, from `sunElevation` and `sunAzimuth`) meets the surface at a point whose normal comes from the stored slope (`normalize(-strength * slope, 1)`). Snell's law at index 1.333 bends it; it travels on until it reaches the floor `depth` below the mean surface, and lands a little to one side. Where neighbouring beams land closer together than they started the floor is brighter, where they spread it is darker, and that ratio of areas is the whole of a caustic. Two ways to measure it, selectable live (`method`):

- **mesh**, the projected light mesh popularised by Evan Wallace's WebGL Water, written here from the idea. A grid of triangles covers the surface (one vertex per solver cell by default, `meshDensity` scales it); the vertex stage fetches the surface, refracts, and places the vertex at its landing point; the fragment stage shades by original area over projected area, taken from the screen-space derivatives of the vertex's position on the surface (`|det(dpdx, dpdy)| * texels`, so a flat surface is exactly 1, not "roughly 1"). Triangles are added together (blend one + one). Because it is additive, a surface that has focused light past the first caustic and folded over itself adds the sheets together, as light does, and energy is conserved by construction: a triangle deposits its original area whatever its shape.
- **jacobian**, a per-pixel backward estimate with no mesh: brightness = `1 / |det(I + dD/dp)|`, where `D(p)` is the sideways travel of the beam from surface point `p`. Each floor texel finds the surface point that sends light to it (three rounds of `p <- q - D(p)`) and measures the Jacobian there with four central differences. One fullscreen pass, no vertices, no blending, no derivatives. It is exact to first order, and it counts one ray per floor point.

Decisions, and why:

- **Resolutions.** The caustics texture is `auto` = half the canvas's long side (clamped to 256..1024): the filaments are one to three texels wide, and a tile floor under a blurred filament looks worse than a slightly soft one at half resolution. The mesh follows the solver's grid, one vertex per cell, so a vertex is about the size of the smallest wave the solver can hold; doubling it (`meshDensity` 2) sharpens the mesh method's filaments and costs vertex work, not fill.
- **Normalisation.** A flat surface gives exactly 1 for both methods (checked to 3% in `tests/sim-caustics.test.ts`) and the mean over a lit floor stays within 1 to 2% of 1 for the mesh on a bump and on a rain-stirred surface: energy is moved, not made. A radiometric factor makes a tilted facet count for the sunlight it actually catches (`l.n / n.z`) and the Fresnel transmission it lets through, both relative to flat. The test with a bump checks the mean and the position of the bright spot.
- **Stability.** The area from derivatives is constant over a triangle and noisy where triangles shrink to slivers. Three guards: a cap per layer (`maxIntensity`, 12), the radiometric weight capped at 4, and an optional light Gaussian blur (`smooth`, sigma in texels). The Jacobian's guard is its regulariser, `1 / sqrt(det^2 + eps^2)` normalised so det = 1 gives 1, with eps 0.15.
- **Light and depth.** `lightDir` and `depth` are parameters of every update. A slanted sun shifts the whole pattern by `depth * tan(refracted angle)` (`flatLandingShift` returns it) and the mesh method covers only the surface that feeds the floor, so the strip that the shift uncovers is lit when `extend` is on (an endless pool, the default) and left in the walls' shadow when it is off (the 3D pool).
- **Occluder.** A sphere (centre, radius, in the solver's frame) shades the floor: for each beam, the distance from the ball's centre to the refracted segment and to the ray toward the sun, turned into a soft edge. It is a smoothstep, so a mesh with one light value per vertex shows no triangles in the shadow's rim. The 3D pool's ball uses it.
- **Chromatic separation.** `colorSplit` sets red's index of refraction to `ior - d` and blue's to `ior + d`. The mesh does three draws, one per channel, into one texture; the Jacobian evaluates three times. Real water's spread is about 0.003; the demo's 0.012 shows fringes without looking like a prism.

**Real against fake.** The `compare` views split the canvas: left is this simulation, right is the procedural `caustics_stack(CAUSTICS_SINE ...)` of `src/wgsl/caustics.wgsl` from Epic 4 at a matching feature size. What you see: the fake covers the whole floor evenly and moves on its own clock, so rain, the cursor, the sun angle and the water depth change nothing about it. The real caustics exist only where the surface is disturbed, are exactly zero where it is flat (a calm surface throws a plain tile floor), draw rings and arcs that follow their wave, shift with the sun, and show the dark halo where a bump spreads the light out. The fake is far nicer for a background nobody touches: no state, no solver, one texture tap if baked.

## Cost

Measured on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal), HUD readings in the browser pane, canvas 1662 x 662 (DPR 2, 1.1 Mpx), solver grid 256 x 102, caustics texture 831 x 331 (`auto`). Indicative: the GPU was shared with another tab, and a pass that draws almost nothing reads about 1 ms here, so the whole effect (solver, caustics and shading) sits at the measurement floor: the frame read 0.9 to 1.2 ms. To get past the floor the `repeat` parameter runs the caustics pass N times per frame; the slope between 1 and 13 repeats gives the cost of one pass (the frame read 0.9 ms at 1, 2.9 to 3.4 ms at 13): jacobian 0.18 ms, mesh 0.17 ms, jacobian with colour split 0.27 ms, mesh with colour split 0.24 ms, mesh at twice the vertex density 0.18 ms; at a 1024 x 408 texture jacobian 0.21 ms, mesh 0.19 ms. One pass is about 3% of the Tier B budget of 6 ms, and either is small beside the solver and the shading pass together. **The prediction that the per-pixel method would be much cheaper than the mesh did not hold on this GPU**: the mesh's rasterisation is cheap at one vertex per solver cell, and the Jacobian's eight refractions per texel are cheap at a quarter megapixel. Not measured: an integrated Intel or AMD GPU, any phone, a browser other than the Chromium-based one in the dev pane. The Jacobian's cost grows with texels, the mesh's with vertices and overdraw, so a phone with a big canvas and a small grid may rank them the other way.

Tier: B. By shape it is a simulation, a caustics pass and a shading pass (three stages, persistent state), which is Tier B's description; by the numbers it would pass Tier A's 2 ms at 1.1 Mpx on this machine, so the verdict rests on the shape until an integrated GPU has measured it. Memory: three `rgba16float` solver textures (0.2 MB each at this size), the caustics texture (2.2 MB) and its blur scratch (2.2 MB), the floor texture (4.4 MB) and the mesh's index buffer (0.6 MB). The HUD reads about 9 MB in total. Bundle size: not measured yet. What scales the cost: the caustics texture size (jacobian), the mesh vertex count (mesh), the canvas size (shading, floor), and the solver's wave speed times grid resolution (steps per frame).

## Pitfalls

- **Beads on the filaments (mesh).** The area is constant over a triangle, so a filament one texel wide is built from triangles, each with its own value, and the result is a necklace. A light blur (`smooth` about 1), more vertices (`meshDensity` 2) or a coarser caustics texture hides it; the Jacobian has no such artefact.
- **Black cores and overshoot where light folds.** Past the first focus the mesh adds the sheets and conserves energy; the Jacobian counts one ray and its mean climbs (1.06 at the default steepness, 1.16 at twice that). Where they differ most is under a fresh strong drop. If the exact energy matters, use the mesh.
- **The strip the shift uncovers.** A slanted sun moves the pattern sideways, and with a surface that stops at the canvas edge the floor strip it uncovers gets no light and shows as a dark band. The demo extends the mesh beyond the domain (and the Jacobian clamps to the edge texel) so the pool never ends; the 3D pool turns that off on purpose because the walls really do shade it.
- **Indexing by floor position, not by pixel.** The caustics are a texture of the FLOOR. Shading has to look them up where the refracted ray lands (`floor_uv`), not at the pixel; looking up at the pixel makes the light slide over the tiles.
- **One uniform buffer for three draws.** `queue.writeBuffer` lands before the whole submit, so a colour-split mesh that updates one buffer between its three draws shows all three the last channel's values. The module keeps one parameter buffer per draw.
- **`hf.view` is re-created with the grid.** Resizing the canvas can change the solver's cell count and with it `heightfield.generation`; the caustics module rebuilds its bind groups on its own, but the shading pass holds the caustics view and must rebuild when `caustics.generation` changes. The demo keys its bind group on both.
- **Glancing light.** A sun below about 3 degrees is clamped (z of the light is kept above 0.05) and a facet turned so far that it grazes the beam transmits nothing and weighs zero; both would otherwise divide by almost nothing.
- **A screenshot lies about time.** A frame 20 ms after a drop shows a small bright dot; the ring needs half a second to read, and the light ring trails the wave.

## When to use it

When light has to answer to the water: a hero, product page or pool-themed section where the cursor or rain stirs the surface and the floor lights up where the waves focus the sun. Tier B on shape (about 1 ms of GPU time at 1.1 Mpx on Apple Silicon). Use `jacobian` for rain, hover and gentle waves, `mesh` when the surface is violent or the energy matters. For a background nobody touches, the procedural caustics of `pool-floor` (a baked map, no solver) are cheaper and nearly as convincing at a glance; the difference only shows when the water is disturbed. For a 3D pool with walls, a floor in perspective and a ball that casts a shadow in the light, see `pool-3d`, which uses this module.
