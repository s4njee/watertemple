## How it works

Broad trails of ink that circle the page's own elements. It is three pieces stacked: the stable-fluids solver of `fluid-dye` (`src/gpu/sim/fluid.ts`), the page measurement of `ripple-dom` (`src/gpu/dom-rects.ts`), and a handful of wandering points, the **heads**, that lay the ink. There is a standalone page built on it, [ink-orbit.html](ink-orbit.html), modelled on hologpu's DOM attractors demo.

**The page.** Elements marked `data-wt-obstacle` are measured once per frame with `getBoundingClientRect` (see the `ripple-dom` notes for why per frame, and what is cached). Each becomes a rounded rectangle in canvas space, and does two things: it is a wall of the solver (`fluid.obstacles.setRects`, re-rasterised only when something moved by a quarter of a cell), and it is a source of force for the heads. A second attribute picks how: `data-wt-orbit="cw"` or `"ccw"` fixes the direction heads are swung round it, `"off"` makes it only push them away (the toolbar of the demo page), and with no attribute directions alternate in the order elements were first seen, so a lane does not flip when an earlier element scrolls away. `dom-rects.ts` gained one field for this: an `Obstacle` now carries the element it was measured from.

**The heads** (`heads.ts`, plain TypeScript, no GPU). Twenty points by default, each with a position and a velocity in image heights. The force on a head is the one hologpu's DOM attractors use for their particles. Per element, with `d` the signed distance to its outline and `n` the outward normal: a spring towards a shell a little outside the edge, `-n * clamp((d - shell) * 5, -1.5, 1)`, plus a constant push along the edge, `spin * tangent * swirl`, both fading as `exp(-5 d)`. It is not gravity; there is no `1 / r^2` and nothing to soften. Drag and a speed limit keep it bounded. On its own that gives a tidy band of points circling each element, so three things untidy it. A slow field of sines pushes every head about (hologpu's ambient drift). No two heads are alike: each has its own shell distance, swirl, drag and top speed. And each is restless: every few seconds it lets go, is pushed off its element for a moment with a shove in a random direction, and is caught by whichever element is nearest when it settles. With several elements pulling at once the paths do not repeat. A head is reborn somewhere else after 12 to 24 seconds, so none of them is hoarded by one card. Heads are kept outside the elements by a hard constraint (projected back out, inward velocity removed), and a soft force turns them back from the canvas edge.

**From heads to ink.** A head is treated exactly like a pointer being dragged through the fluid (`fluid-stir.ts`): along the path it took this frame it queues one capsule of dye and one of momentum (`fluid.addDye`, `fluid.addForce`, with the smooth end ramps that make successive capsules join into one unbroken stroke). The colour is one ink of the palette from head to tail (`primary`: red, yellow, blue, green, each letting at least one channel of the paper through untouched) or, with the cosine palettes, drifts along the trail. The dye is what you see; the momentum (`wake`, a fraction of the head's velocity) is why the trail does not just sit there: the projection turns each push into a pair of counter-rotating vortices at the sides of the stroke, and the tail curls, spreads and is torn by the next head passing. A push as broad as the stroke only carries ink along, though, and the colours slide past each other without mixing. So each head also drives a narrow jet through the middle of its stroke whose direction swings from side to side about once a second, like a fish's tail (`stir`): every swing sheds a vortex a fraction of the stroke's width across, alternately left and right, and that row of vortices is what folds one trail's ink into its neighbours'. Vorticity confinement keeps those vortices alive and a low velocity dissipation lets them keep working. A head that dawdles lays less ink, so it does not soak one spot. The solver stores no dye inside a wall, so a trail much broader than the gap it is in simply shows where there is water.

**Why the pull acts on the heads and not on the water.** The obvious way to make ink orbit an element is a force field on the fluid. An incompressible fluid cancels the pulling part of such a field completely: a force towards a card is a pure gradient, and the pressure solve removes exactly that. Only the turning part survives. `orbit.wgsl` is that part, kept small: one compute pass on the solver's `hooks.forces` that relaxes the velocity towards a slow flow along the level sets of each element's distance field (`current`, 0.05 image heights per second by default). Such a flow is exactly divergence free, so the projection leaves it alone, and relaxing (rather than pushing) is unconditionally stable. It keeps the ink turning after its head has left. The same pass adds a moving element's own velocity near its edge, which is how a dragged card carries the water with it. The pass is skipped when `current` is 0 and nothing is moving.

**One shared head colour.** The default `rgb cycle` palette smoothly fades red → green → blue → red over six seconds on the effect's simulation clock. The fade blends linear RGB light so its energy stays constant through each transition. Every head and pointer lays new ink in that same current colour. The dye stores the colour when it is laid, so the bodies retain their earlier colours as they flow, mix and fade. Pause freezes the cycle, Reset restarts it, and reduced motion holds red. The other palettes remain available in the controls.

**Ink on paper** (`display.wgsl`). On the light ground the dye is optical density per channel: it adds in the solver and the paper is multiplied by `exp(-density)`, so colours filter each other like real ink. Honest subtractive mixing takes a pile of every hue to black, and broad trails pile up constantly, so the shading has two soft shoulders: the absorbance common to all three channels saturates early, and what is left saturates on its strongest channel with the hue kept. Thin ink is unchanged; red over green is a deep yellow instead of mud. The default dark ground is emissive, as in `fluid-dye`: RGB light adds where trails overlap, with two rings of neighbouring samples providing a soft halo. Neutral light is reduced at RGB overlaps and peak emission leaves room for the halo so bright mixtures keep their saturation. The demo uses brighter, narrower trails and disables wet sheen for a lighting appearance; the light ground remains available for subtractive ink.

**HDR.** The effect asks for an extended-range canvas (`hdr: true`; the host gives one where the display and browser can, and only when the page allows it). Then the whole picture, including emissive RGB trails on the dark ground, is multiplied by `paperLight`, 1.5 by default. On the light ground: the paper is one and a half times SDR white, and the ink, which only ever takes light away from the paper, is as saturated and as bright as the display allows. No CSS colour is that bright, so page elements cannot match the paper with a background of their own. `cutout` solves that from the other side: the shading pass draws every measured element as clean paper, from its exact rounded rectangle and to the pixel, and the page leaves those elements transparent. On an SDR canvas `paperLight` does nothing.

**The first frame.** The heads are run forward for a moment on the CPU and the trails they would have left are laid at once (dye only), so the page is not blank when it appears, or when it is held paused for a visitor who prefers reduced motion.

**The demo page.** `ink-orbit.html` and `src/demos/ink-orbit/` are an ordinary page using the effect through `mountEffect`: a fixed canvas behind the content, luminous RGB trails over a dark background and opaque dark plates, a draggable card, and a toolbar that changes parameters and flips the `data-wt-orbit` attributes. On an HDR display it turns `cutout` on and makes its text plates transparent; the card and the toolbar stay opaque SDR white, which is what shows how much brighter the paper round them is. The canvas is behind the content and never receives the pointer, so the page forwards pointer events to it; presses on text and controls are not forwarded, so selecting and clicking still work.

## Cost

Indicative reading from the HUD on the development laptop (Chromium), 1742 x 1020 drawing buffer (1.8 Mpx, DPR 2), default parameters, SDR canvas, bare Canvas layout with the three demo cards: 120 fps, GPU about 2.3 ms per frame (shade about 2.3, dye about 2.1, advect and forces about 0.7, pressure about 0.6, the current pass about 0.02; the passes overlap, so they do not add up), CPU about 0.3 ms. Not measured: an HDR canvas (docs/effect-authoring.md records a large drop in frame rate for the whole page on this machine with one), the demo page's 768-texel dye grid over a full viewport, any other device. The scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty for that reason.

What it runs per frame is the `fluid-dye` solver at the same default grids (128 velocity cells and 512 dye texels on the short side, 30 pressure iterations), plus one small compute pass over the velocity grid per simulation step (a loop over at most 24 boxes per face), plus one fullscreen shading pass. The heads cost a few microseconds of JavaScript (twenty points, a loop over the elements each) and at most 48 splats of each kind, of the solver's 256. The page measurement is `ripple-dom`'s.

## Pitfalls

- A very broad trail hides most of the page's paper. The demo page uses a 32 px brush for luminous ribbons with the elements cut out. `inkRadius` down to about 0.01 gives lines again (the `swarm` preset).
- Elements are rectangles: text is the box of its element, a rotated element is its bounding box (see `ripple-dom`). The heads use the same boxes, so they circle the box, not the glyphs.
- The mask is as coarse as the velocity grid (about 6 px per cell at 128 cells over an 800 px viewport), so ink stops within a cell of an element's edge rather than exactly on it. Opaque plates hide the difference; a transparent element shows a thin clean margin.
- The heads live in canvas space, not page space. When the page scrolls, elements slide past them: a head that an element moves onto is pushed out of the way, and the trail is not drawn across that jump.
- Elements packed closer together than the shell distance leave a head nowhere to settle and it jitters between them. Keep `shell` below half the smallest gap that matters.
- Heads are deterministic for a given seed and page, but frame-rate independent only approximately: the integration is cut into steps of at most 1/60 s, not run on a fixed clock.
- The solver clears its ink when the canvas changes shape by more than about 6 percent (it re-creates its grids); the first trails are then laid again.
- An HDR canvas is brighter than everything else on the page, including the page's own white. That is the point, and it is also a lot of light: keep `paperLight` modest, and give visitors a way to switch it off (the demo page has an SDR option).
- With `cutout` on, the cut-outs follow the page one frame late (they are drawn from the last measurement), so during a fast scroll text can sit over ink for a frame. Opaque plates hide that; transparent ones do not.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` slows the heads inside the effect; the demo page goes further and starts paused, with Play as the opt-in.

## When to use it

Tier B, compute only: a hero or a whole landing page where the content should feel like it sits in water, and where a few seconds of looking is rewarded. It suits a light page unusually well, because subtractive ink on paper stays readable in a way additive glow on white cannot. It needs elements with room between them; a dense dashboard gives the heads nowhere to go. For water that only reacts to the page (no ink, no autonomy, Tier A, no compute) use `ripple-dom`. For ink without the page, `fluid-dye`. Without WebGPU the page is unchanged, because the elements are only measured: show the paper colour, or a few soft gradients behind the content.
