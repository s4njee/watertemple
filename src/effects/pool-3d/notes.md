## How it works

A tiled pool in a deck, seen in perspective, an orbit camera, a ball you can drag through the water, ripples from clicks and rain, and a light show on the floor that comes from the ripples themselves. A deliberate homage to the classic interactive pool demo (Evan Wallace's WebGL Water), built from this project's own parts: the heightfield solver (`src/gpu/sim/heightfield.ts`), the caustics module (`src/gpu/sim/caustics.ts`, see `ripple-caustics`), the scene camera and orbit maths (`src/gpu/scene/`) and the optics chunks in `src/wgsl/`.

Per frame, three stages. **Solver**: the same 2D wave equation as `ripples`, on a square grid that covers the pool whatever the shape of the canvas. **Caustics**: one pass turns that surface into a floor-space light texture, with the ball as an occluder and `extend` off, so the pool's walls really do shade a strip of the floor. **Scene**: one render pass draws the sky, the deck, the pool's inside, the ball and the water.

**What is rasterised.** A sky (a fullscreen triangle, depth ignored), the deck (a big quad at the rim height with the pool's opening cut by `discard`), the pool's inside (a unit box with its top face removed, stretched to the pool and drawn with the camera-facing side culled, so the near walls are cut away), the ball (a unit sphere moved and scaled in the vertex stage) and the water surface (a grid, `meshResolution` cells a side, with no vertex buffer: the vertex index is the grid position, and the vertex stage lifts each vertex by the solver's height). All five are in one module, `pool.wgsl`.

**What is ray traced.** Only the water, and only against two analytic shapes. For each water fragment the shader reads the solver's slope at that pixel (one bilinear tap, so the normal is smoother than the mesh), reflects and refracts the view ray with Snell's law at 1.333 and weights the two by Schlick Fresnel (`fresnel.wgsl`). Each ray is then intersected in closed form (`pool-rays.wgsl`): with the ball by a ray-sphere test, and with the pool by the exit point of an open box (`pl_exit`: the nearest of four walls and the floor or the open top). The reflected ray sees the ball, the walls above the waterline, or the sky (`sky.wgsl`, sun disc included, so a wave facet that faces the sun glints exactly where it should). The refracted ray sees the ball or the wall or floor below the surface. A hit is shaded and the ray ends: one bounce.

**Shading under water.** Tile albedo (`backdrop_pool_tiles`, tiled in world space on the floor and walls) times the light that gets there: sky fill that fades with depth, plus the sun, which arrives bent by the surface (the flat refraction of the sun direction), attenuated by Beer-Lambert along its slanted path, and modulated by the live caustics. The result is absorbed and scattered along the path the eye ray took (`absorb_composite`), so deeper means bluer. The caustics texture is indexed by floor position: a floor point looks itself up; a wall or the ball slides along the refracted sun ray down to floor depth first, which is an approximation (the pattern on a wall is the floor's pattern shifted, not its own focus), and a point whose beam would have entered outside the pool is dark. The ball's shadow on the floor is the occluder in the caustics pass (soft edge, both the part above and below the water); its shadow on the walls above water is one ray-sphere test per fragment.

**Interaction.** The camera is `stepOrbit` fed from `trackPointers` (so it can tell where a press lands before deciding what it is). A press first asks the CPU ray-sphere test whether it hit the ball: if so the drag moves the ball in a plane facing the camera through the grab point (`beginDrag`, `dragOnPlane`, with the grab offset kept so it does not jump); if not, the drag orbits. A press and release with little travel and no time is a click and makes a drop where the ray meets the water. The ball has a little physics (`stepBall`): buoyancy from the submerged fraction of its volume against gravity, drag that grows with the submerged part, a floor; released above water it falls and splashes. It stirs the water two ways: its horizontal motion through `Heightfield.displace` (once per frame, with the radius of the circle it cuts at the waterline, fading with depth) and the change in submerged volume as a dent or a bump over its footprint through `addDrop`, so a plunge makes a splash and a wake follows a drag. Every pointer is captured during a drag and `touch-action` is set to none (and restored on destroy), because this is a showpiece, not a background. Left alone the camera drifts and the water gets an occasional drop.

**What it costs to cut.** `quality: cheap` reflects only the sky and leaves the ball out of the refraction trace; the `cheap cut` preset also drops the caustics to the Jacobian method at 256 texels, the grid to 128 and the mesh to 64.

## Cost

Measured in the browser pane on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal), HUD readings, indicative: the GPU was shared with another tab, and a pass that draws almost nothing reads about 1 ms here, so everything below sits just above that floor. Canvas 1662 x 662 (1.1 Mpx, DPR 2); defaults (grid 192, mesh 128, caustics 512 by the mesh method): frame GPU 1.24 to 1.41 ms, of which the scene pass about 0.7 to 0.8 ms and the caustics pass about 0.2 ms. Against canvas size (the HUD's resolution scale) the frame read 1.03 ms at 0.07 Mpx, 1.21 ms at 0.28 Mpx, 1.33 ms at 0.62 Mpx and 1.41 ms at 1.1 Mpx, a slope of about 0.4 ms per megapixel, which puts 1080p (2.1 Mpx) at roughly 1.8 ms: an extrapolation, not a measurement. Caustics off: 1.23 ms. The `cheap cut` preset: 1.15 ms. Everything at its maximum (grid 384, mesh 192, caustics 1024 by the mesh method): 2.11 ms. All of them are well inside the Tier B budget of 6 ms.

Tier: B. By shape it is a full 3D scene with a simulation and a per-pixel ray trace, which sounds like Tier C, but the scene is a handful of triangles, the ray trace is two analytic intersections per pixel and the whole frame extrapolates to under 2 ms at 1080p here, so the plan's own tier for this story (B) holds on Apple Silicon. That is the only device it has been measured on: the water fragment shader does two ray traces, a dozen exponentials and a sky evaluation per pixel, which an integrated GPU or a phone may find a lot heavier than this laptop does, and the `cheap` quality exists for that case. It has not been run on a phone. Memory: three `rgba16float` solver textures (0.9 MB at 192 cells), the caustics texture and its blur scratch (4.2 MB at 512), a depth buffer (4.4 MB at 1.1 Mpx) and two index buffers (about 1.3 MB). What scales the cost: the canvas size (the water shader), the solver's grid times wave speed (steps per frame), the caustics texture size and the surface mesh. Bundle size: not measured yet.

## Pitfalls

- **Single bounce.** A reflected ray that hits the ball shows the ball lit by the sun and the sky, not the ball's own reflection of the water, and a refracted ray that hits the ball shows no second refraction through it (the ball is opaque). Light that bounces off the floor onto the walls is missing. This is what analytic tracing costs: every extra bounce is another trace of the same shapes, and every extra shape another test.
- **Only the shapes you wrote.** The box and the sphere are in the shader as formulas. A second ball is another sphere test; a pool with a bench or a rounded corner is a new intersector. There is no general scene to trace; anything that is not one of them is invisible to the reflections and refractions (the deck, the sky and the rim are, because the rays leave through the top).
- **The camera cannot go under the water.** Seen from below, the surface needs total internal reflection and a window of sky, which the shader does not have, so the camera is clamped above it: the orbit stops at a pitch of 0.28 rad (about 16 degrees), which also keeps the eye above the deck's rim.
- **The deck hides the near part of the pool at low angles.** It is real geometry, so a ball dragged to the near wall can go behind it. Orbit up. A cutaway deck would fix it and would cost the picture its sense of place.
- **Waves do not reflect off the ball.** The ball pushes water (`displace`, `addDrop`) but is not an obstacle in the solver's mask, which today takes rectangles; a ring runs straight through it.
- **Wall and ball caustics are borrowed from the floor.** Good enough that nobody looks twice, wrong in detail. The sun's own wall shadows come out right because the beam either entered the pool or did not.
- **The shadow side is dark.** The walls shade a strip of the floor at any slanted sun, a quarter to a half of it at low sun, and the sky fill under water is faint. That is correct and harsh; the `low sun` preset shows it, and `high noon` shows the opposite.
- **A click that is over in one frame.** The first version took a press only if the pointer was still down when it polled, so a tap shorter than a frame (a synthetic click, a fast touch) did nothing. A press and its release in one poll is a click, and the gesture starts on `justPressed`.
- **Derivatives and branches.** The water shader needs the pixel footprint to antialias the tiles it traces (`fwidth` of the surface point), and WGSL only allows that in uniform control flow, so it is computed first, before any `discard` or branch. The antialias is an estimate (the footprint scaled by the path length), good to hide shimmer, not exact.
- **No multisampling.** Edges (the rim, the ball against the water) alias at low density; the tile shimmer is handled in the shader. MSAA would be a second pipeline set and a resolve for a modest gain.
- **Sun glints are single pixels.** The sky's sun disc is about 20 mrad across and a pixel is about 1 mrad, so a facet that reflects it produces a one-pixel sparkle that twinkles as the waves move. Realistic, and a little noisy on a still screenshot.
- **One `update` per submitted frame, resize wipes the water.** Inherited from the solver (see `ripples`). Changing the grid resolution re-creates the surface flat; the effect keeps the idle drops going so it refills.

## When to use it

A page where the water is the subject and the visitor is meant to play with it: a landing page for a pool or spa product, a configurator, a game-like hero, a demo of what the site can do. Tier B as measured (about 1.4 ms at 1.1 Mpx on Apple Silicon), desktop first; the `cheap cut` preset and the `quality: cheap` option are the dial for weaker GPUs. It is not a background: it takes the pointer, so text over it cannot be clicked through. For a decorative floor under content, use `ripple-caustics` (two passes' worth of 2D, same caustics module) or the baked `pool-floor`. For an ocean or a lake the analytic scene is the wrong tool: the shapes are a box and a sphere.
