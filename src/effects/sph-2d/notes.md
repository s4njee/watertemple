## How it works

A tank of water as particles, the way Müller, Charypar and Gross did it in 2003 and the weakly compressible literature has refined it since. Three pieces: the solver (`src/gpu/sim/sph.ts` and `sph-*.wgsl`), the kernels (`src/wgsl/sph-kernels.wgsl`), and the drawing (`src/gpu/liquid-2d/`, usable for any 2D particle set). The effect (`index.ts`) is the scenes, the pointer and the plumbing.

**One sub-step is two compute passes, plus a grid build on some of them.** The grid (`src/gpu/particles/neighbor-grid.ts`, reorder mode) bins the particles and physically sorts positions and velocities into cell order, which is what makes the following passes fast. Then `sph-density.wgsl` sums `rho_i = m * sum W_poly6(r_ij)` over the neighbours and turns it into a pressure; `sph-force.wgsl` sums the forces, integrates (semi-implicit Euler: velocity first, then position) and projects back into the box, writing to the other half of a double buffer.

- **Equation of state.** Tait, `p = B ((rho / rho0)^7 - 1)`, `B = rho0 c^2 / 7`, so `c` is the speed of sound at rest. Negative pressure is clamped to zero. Without that the free surface clumps (the tensile instability: particles snap into pairs and strings); with it a surface particle simply feels the bulk pressure and nothing pulls it back. Surface tension is a separate, explicit term.
- **Forces.** Pressure with the symmetric form `m (p_i / rho_i^2 + p_j / rho_j^2) grad W_spiky`; laminar viscosity from the kernel's Laplacian (`nu` is a true kinematic viscosity in m²/s; the 2D constant is derived from the consistency condition of a discrete Laplacian, 40/(pi h^5), not borrowed from the 3D 45/(pi h^6)); Monaghan's artificial viscosity between approaching pairs, which is what damps the sound waves; a pairwise cohesion for surface tension; gravity, linear damping, and the pointer. Every pair force is equal and opposite, so total momentum is conserved to round-off (measured: a drift of 1.4e-8 of the summed speeds over 48 sub-steps with random velocities and everything switched on).
- **Kernels.** poly6 for density, the gradient of the spiky kernel for pressure (poly6's gradient vanishes at the centre, so close particles would not repel), the viscosity Laplacian. The 3D constants in most references are wrong in 2D: the integral carries `2 pi r dr`, not `4 pi r^2 dr`. The 2D and 3D constants are derived, and `tests/sph.test.ts` integrates each kernel on the GPU to 1 within 1e-3 (the 3D poly6 constant used in 2D integrates to the wrong number, which is also tested), checks every gradient against finite differences and the viscosity consistency condition by quadrature.
- **Rest density from the lattice.** Particles spawn on a hexagonal lattice of spacing `s`, support radius `h = 2.4 s`, mass `m = 1000 s²`. The rest density in the equation of state is not `1000` but the density the kernel sum reads on that lattice (993.2 at this ratio, 0.7 percent lower), so a block at lattice spacing is at zero pressure by construction: the interior of a block reads rest density to 3e-6 (float round-off), and the same block on a square lattice reads within a percent. To change resolution only the spacing changes: `h` and `m` follow it, every physical parameter is independent of it, and the time step shrinks with it.
- **Boundaries are a continuum, not particles.** Penalty forces make a layer of particles stick to the floor; boundary particles cost a layer per wall and per obstacle. Here the wall is treated as fluid at rest density filling the half-plane behind it, and what that fluid would contribute is integrated analytically: its density (the integral of the poly6 kernel beyond distance `d`) and its pressure force (the line integral of the spiky kernel, by the divergence theorem), both tabulated on the CPU (`sphWallTables`, checked against a brute-force 2D quadrature). Obstacles (circle, rounded box) use their SDF as a locally flat wall. On top of that a hard projection after every step puts any particle back inside the box and out of the obstacles with restitution and friction, so "nothing leaves the box" holds whatever the forces did, with a violent pointer impulse and gravity of 1e4 tested. What it looks like: a settled column stands half a spacing off the floor, the bottom layer's density is within 3 percent of the layer above, and the side-wall layer within 4 percent of the interior (no crust, no gap).
- **Time step.** `dt <= courant h / c` with `c = stiffness * sqrt(g H)` (free-fall speed over the domain height, floored at 2 m/s), plus force and viscous limits; the number of sub-steps per 1/60 s tick is the integer that satisfies it, so `dt` is exactly `(1/60)/n` and a 120 Hz display runs `n/2` per frame. The stability limit was measured on the dam break: courant 0.8 is stable and 1.0 is not, so the default is 0.5. Default stiffness 4 compresses a column of the domain's height by about 6 percent (1/stiffness²); the 0.5 m column in the tests settles 0.9 percent compressed on average. Sub-steps per frame at the demo's tank: 12 at 2 000 particles, 27 at 10 000 (the default 12 000: about 30), 38 at 20 000, 60 at 50 000, 84 at 100 000, 132 at 250 000.
- **Grid skin.** The grid can be rebuilt every `K` steps with a cell side of `h + (K-1) speed_max dt`; the search stays exact because the speed is clamped (a test compares K = 8 with a brute-force search to 1e-4). At the default clamp (0.4 c) that allows K = 1 to 2, so in practice the grid is rebuilt every other step; the cost tables below say what that buys.

**Drawing** (`src/gpu/liquid-2d/`). Discs look like ball bearings. Every particle is splatted as a soft blob (a compactly supported `(1 - q²)³` kernel, normalised so bulk water at rest density is 1.0 in the field) into a half-density field, blurred a little, and a fullscreen pass thresholds it at 0.5 with an antialiased edge, takes the normal from the gradient of the field and of a wide copy of it (so the body reads as a gently domed surface), colours it by thickness with the shared Beer–Lambert chunk, adds a rim, a highlight, refraction of the tiled wall behind it, and foam where the particle's speed is high. The per-particle scalar `positions.w` carries the speed in the liquid view and the density ratio or the normalised pressure in the other views. The obstacles, the glass and the pointer ring are drawn over the liquid by `overlay.wgsl`.

**The effect.** Three scenes, each restartable (flip "Restart", or change the scene or the count): a dam break (a 0.55 × 0.95 m column in a 2 × 1.2 m tank), a pour (a nozzle drops a stream onto a shelf, with a rock in the tank; the solver's emitter releases rows of particles at exactly the lattice density), and two blobs in zero gravity that merge and round off under surface tension (the preset sets gravity to 0). Press and drag to push the water (the drag force is scaled by the pointer's speed, so a still pointer does nothing); the mode can be switched to pull. A quick tap (under 0.35 s, with almost no movement) drops a round block of water there. **Touch policy:** the canvas does not capture the pointer by default, so a full-width canvas on a phone stays scrollable and only taps (block drops) reach the water; "Touch drags push the water" turns `touch-action: none` and pointer capture on for pages where the water is the point. Gravity has a direction (`tilt`, the container-roll story's hook). Views: the liquid, plain discs (the baseline), density, pressure, speed.

Reading it. **The frame cost is the sub-step count times the cost of a step, and both grow with the particle count**: the steps because the spacing shrinks (more particles means a smaller `h`, so a smaller `dt`), the step because there is more to do. Up to roughly 10 000 particles a step is limited by dispatch latency, not work (three dependent passes cost about 0.15 ms whatever the count), so the frame is about `sub-steps × 0.15 ms`, 3 to 4 ms; from 50 000 the passes are bandwidth-bound and the frame grows with the particle count to a power of about 1.5 to 2. The neighbour search is not the problem (the build is 0.04 to 0.16 ms); the explicit method's step count is. Rebuilding the grid every second step instead of every step saves about a third at 20 000 particles (the two columns differ by less than the noise above 50 000). **Numbers from this study are about twice the browser's**: the study ran with the load average at 16 to 28 and, across three runs, the worst run is up to three times the best. In the browser (Chrome pane, DPR 2, 968×976, the whole frame including drawing, indicative, on a quieter machine): 20 000 particles 115 fps at 4.4 ms GPU, 40 000 particles 96 fps at 7.4 ms, 60 000 particles 22 fps at 40 ms (read while other GPU work was running; I could not repeat it, the browser later lost its WebGPU device). **The largest count that held 60 fps in the browser is 40 000 particles; the noisy study would say 20 000 to 30 000.** Plan on 10 000 to 20 000 with margin. 100 000 particles is 70 to 80 ms and 250 000 particles 130 to 220 ms per frame by the study: the 84 and 132 sub-steps per frame are the price of the explicit method. The drawing is about 0.8 ms (splat 0.1 to 0.3, wide map 0.2, composite 0.3, overlay 0.4 in the HUD, which overlaps). Memory: 64 bytes per particle in the particle store and its double buffers, 8 for the density state and about 12 for the grid, so 60 000 particles are under 6 MB.

Tier: **B**, from the measurement: at the default 12 000 particles (about 30 sub-steps a frame) the frame is a few milliseconds of GPU time on Apple Silicon (budget 6 ms at 1080p; the browser read 4.4 ms at 20 000), compute only, no WebGL2 path. It is not a Tier A effect and not a phone effect without cutting the count to a few thousand; the reference devices beyond the M1 Max are not measured (`scorecard.mobile` is `untested`).

## Pitfalls

- **Compressibility bounce.** The fluid is weakly compressible by construction: a column starts at rest density and compresses by `g H / c²` the instant gravity acts, which rings as a sound wave. Measured: 2 percent at the bottom of a 0.5 m column at the default stiffness, and a residual speed of 5 mm/s rms after 5 s (peak 5 cm/s). The damping that does it is viscosity: with a viscosity of 0.02 m²/s and a weaker artificial viscosity the same column still jittered at 0.2 m/s peak after 4 s (disorder noise of the particle lattice, not a time-step effect: halving the step changed nothing), and at 0.1 m²/s it was quiet. Stiffer is smoother and slower (sub-steps scale with it); the demo's `gravityRamp` hook is there for scenes that want a soft start.
- **Clumping.** Removed by the negative-pressure clamp, at the price of a free surface with no pressure holding it: spray detaches readily, and at a thin stream (a few particles across) the particles are all "surface" and the stream disintegrates into beads as it stretches in the fall. The pour scene uses a wider, faster stream and a little cohesion to hold it together. A cohesion that is strong enough to round a blob in a second (200 m/s²) also keeps a few particles from ever leaving it.
- **Boundaries.** The analytic wall assumes a locally flat wall: curved obstacles are fine at several kernel radii, the very corner of the box over-counts (two half-planes overlap there), so the corner particle sits a hair further out. The hard projection is the safety net, not the mechanism; a particle that reaches it has already had its velocity damped by the restitution, so a violent hit loses energy there.
- **Resolution costs sub-steps.** Halving the spacing doubles the sub-steps per frame (the CFL rule), so four times the particles is about eight times the work. The solver caps them (128 by default); above the cap the step is larger than stable and only the velocity clamp and the pressure cap keep it from blowing up. Rendering is cheap; it is never the limit.
- **Laminar viscosity is capped** at what the explicit step can carry (`nu <= 0.12 h² / dt`), so at high resolution the honey preset is less viscous than asked; the artificial viscosity is the damping that stays.
- **A tap into water injects overlapping fluid.** The density cap on the equation of state (1.25× rest) keeps the push finite, so it makes a splash and a crater rather than an explosion, but it is not mass-conserving.
- **Particle order changes every step or two.** The grid reorders all particle data; nothing here follows individual particles. A test or an effect that needs identity must keep its own id attribute.
- **Field resolution.** On large canvases the effect halves the field resolution; below about 2 field texels per particle spacing the surface scallops at the lattice scale, and the blur sigma follows the spacing for that reason.
- The HUD times only the first and last sub-step of a frame (a hundred sub-steps exceed the profiler's slots, and summing overlapping intervals would be wrong); the frame span it reports still covers the whole simulation.

## When to use it

A body of water that has to splash, pour, slosh and be pushed around on desktop-class GPUs, at about ten to forty thousand particles: a hero tank, a loading illustration that fills a vessel, a droplet that merges with another, a toy. Tier B. For a flat, calm surface use the heightfield (Epic 5); for ink and smoke the grid fluid (Epic 6), which costs the same at any resolution of detail; for a water body with a real free surface at higher counts reach for position-based fluids (story 7.4), which take far fewer, larger steps for the price of more passes per step, or for MLS-MPM in 3D. Particle SPH is the right tool when the splashes and the free surface are the subject and the count can stay low.
