## How it works

Pigment on paper is a stable-fluids solver with a second layer on top. The solver (`src/gpu/sim/fluid.ts`, see the `fluid-dye` notes) carries the pigment as its dye on a fine grid, by a coarse velocity grid. This effect adds what ink does that smoke does not, as one reusable module, `src/gpu/sim/pigment.ts`, installed through the solver's two hooks (`hooks.dye`, `hooks.forces`), plus a shading pass.

**What is stored.** The dye texture (the solver's, `rgba16float`) holds the **suspended** pigment as optical density (absorbance) per channel, `a` holding its mean for buoyancy. Two textures of the pigment layer, both on the dye grid and ping-ponged, hold the paper's state: the **stain** (`rgba16float`: rgb the deposited density, `a` the shaped wetness for shading) and the **wetness** (`r32float`, 0 to 1, the film of water; 32 bit because drying moves it by less than a half float can represent). The stain is never advected: once pigment has left the water it stays where it settled. A one-texel-per-texel paper tile (`paper.wgsl`, `wash_paper_tile` from `wash.wgsl`, 256 squared, repeating) gives grain and fibre to the simulation and to the shading alike.

**Passes per solver step** (one 60 Hz step per frame in the demo):

| Pass                                         | Grid                           | What it does                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| solver: advect velocity, forces, viscosity   | velocity (64 cells short side) | the stable-fluids step; the brush's momentum went in with the splats                            |
| `pigment-flow.wgsl` (forces hook)            | velocity                       | dry paper holds the water back: `v *= exp(-drag (1 - wet) dt)`; skipped when submerged          |
| solver: pressure projection                  | velocity                       | 24 Jacobi iterations, as in `fluid-dye`                                                         |
| solver: advect dye (MacCormack or semi-Lag.) | dye (384 short side)           | the pigment is carried by the water                                                             |
| `pigment-step.wgsl` `cs_step` (dye hook)     | dye                            | diffusion, edge drift, settling, lifting, fading and the wetness update, in ONE dispatch        |
| `pigment-step.wgsl` `cs_transport` x (n - 1) | dye                            | extra diffusion sub-steps, only when the diffusivity needs them (see below)                     |
| `pigment-water.wgsl` (once per frame)        | dye                            | the brush's water: capsule splats of wetness, ragged by two octaves of noise fixed to the sheet |
| `display.wgsl`                               | canvas                         | paper, granulation, Beer-Lambert composite, wet sheen, debug views                              |

**Diffusion** is a real diffusion term, explicit and conservative: across each face between two texels the pigment exchanges `k (c_neighbour - c)` with `k = min(D dt / dx^2 * wetter * paper, 0.12)`. `D` is the diffusivity in image heights squared per second (a parameter); `wetter` is `mix(wicking, 1, mean shaped wetness of the two texels)`, so pigment spreads in a wet film and hardly on dry paper; `paper` is `1 + feather (3 fibre + 1.6 (grain - 0.5))` of the two texels, so a fibre is a fast lane (feathering). `k` is the same for both texels of a face, so what one loses the other gains: the total is conserved exactly (in 32-bit floats, to 1e-3 in the tests), the variance of a point grows by `2k` per step and axis, **linearly in time and with the coefficient the parameter says, whatever the step size** (the tests run 1/30, 1/60 and 1/240 s and the continuum value 2 D T is hit within 1 percent). The cap of 0.12 per face is the stability limit of the scheme; a diffusivity above `0.12 / (dt H^2)` (H the dye height in texels, 5e-5 heights squared per second at 384) is split into up to `maxSubsteps` equal sub-steps (a dispatch each) and saturates beyond that instead of blowing up. This was chosen over implicit Jacobi relaxation (unconditionally stable but not conservative with a variable coefficient, and it smears faster than it should in few iterations) and over a separable Gaussian (exact, but a wide kernel per step and no variable coefficient).

**Edge drift (edge darkening, blooms).** Where the film thins, water is drawn out towards the drier paper and carries pigment: the bloom of a drop on wet paper, and, where a flat-topped film ends, the dark ring of a dried wash. Across a face the pigment moves `edge_v * smoothstep(0.3 g0, g0, |grad w|) * (w_a - w_b) / |grad w|` texels per step from the wetter to the drier texel, as an upwind flux (conservative, positive), with `|grad w|` the gradient of the raw wetness at the face (the normal difference and the mean of the two texels' central differences along the face). The pigment is then taken out of the water by the next rule as the paper under it gets drier, so it stays where the drift left it: the ring.

**Settling (absorption into paper, granulation, back-runs).** Suspended pigment `c` moves into the stain at `kd = absorb * mix(1, wetAbsorption, shaped wetness) * gran`, `1 - exp(-kd dt)` of it per step: fast on dry paper, slow under water (a wet-on-wet wash stays mobile, a dry brush stains where it lands). `gran = 1 + granulation * 1.4 * (0.5 - grain)` makes the paper's valleys take more than its peaks. Stain in a wet film dissolves again at `lift * wetness^2`: a clean-water brush over a dried wash lifts pigment (the lighter band and darker edges of a back-run). Both moves are one subtraction and one addition of the same number, so `suspended + stain` is conserved by this pass too (tests: to 1e-3 in 32-bit floats).

**Wetness** spreads a little (capillary creep), then dries: times `exp(-drying dt)` minus a small linear part so it reaches zero. The exponential, not a plain subtraction, is deliberate: the extent of a wash then shrinks only slowly, so the film's edge stays put while its thickness falls, which is meant to keep the pigment the drift has gathered at the rim (the alternative was not tried). `Submerged` is a floor under the wetness: at 1 the whole sheet is under water, nothing dries, nothing drags the flow: ink in a pool.

**Colour** is Beer-Lambert in `composite.wgsl` (the one function the display pass and the tests share): the paper is multiplied by `exp(-(suspended + stain))`. Densities add, so transmittances multiply: blue and yellow make green, equal parts of two inks are the geometric mean of their colours, and nothing is ever lighter than the paper in any channel (a test runs 2000 random densities through the GPU function). Inks are named by the colour a medium layer transmits; the density of the pigment is its negative logarithm. On the dark paper preset a pigment cannot darken the sheet, so the density is read as opacity and the pigment's own hue glows over it: a luminous (gouache or neon) ink, not the same physics, with the same mixing rule.

**Interaction** (`index.ts`). A press-drag paints: the solver's stirrer (`fluid-stir.ts`) lays capsules of momentum and ink, and the same capsules wet the paper (clean water is a brush whose ink has no colour). Pen pressure widens the stroke, speed thins and lightens it. A press is also a dab that blooms: a drop of ink in a patch of water with a few outward pushes, so a tap is a drop. A brush held still keeps wetting its patch. Idle drops land every few seconds and now and then a slow stroke is drawn, so the sheet is never dead; they ease off while someone is painting and slow down under `prefers-reduced-motion`. **Touch drags scroll the page** unless `Touch drags` is set to `paints` (then the canvas takes the pointer, `touch-action: none`), the policy of `fluid-dye` and `ripples`; a mouse only paints while pressed (hovering never paints, since a watercolour should not smear from a passing cursor); `Pointer: hover stirs` lets a hover push the water without ink.

**Parameters worth knowing:** `Diffusion`, `Feathering`, `Viscosity`, `Absorption` (dry brush against wet-on-wet), `Drying speed`, `Edge darkening`, `Granulation`, `Back-runs`, `Submerged`, `Paper`, `Wet sheen`, and the debug views (wetness, deposited, suspended, velocity). Presets: wet-on-wet watercolour (the default), ink drop in water, sumi-e, granulating wash, night ink.

## Cost

Indicative, from GPU timestamp queries in `tests/ink-water.test.ts` (run `INKWATER_STUDY=/tmp/inkwater-study.txt npx vitest run tests/ink-water.test.ts -t study`): Apple Silicon laptop (Metal through Dawn), canvas 1662 x 932 (the browser pane at DPR 2), velocity grid 64 cells and dye grid 384 on the short side (683 x 384), 24 pressure iterations. The span is the frame's first timed pass start to its last end, and the cost per step is the slope between a low and a high number of steps (the ~1 ms floor of a pass removes itself, see `docs/effect-authoring.md`), minimum of 9 submits. **The GPU was shared with other work while these ran (other agents' tests), and a first run with medians was off by a factor of five; the minimum is the least wrong estimate but the figures below are good to about plus or minus 0.5 ms.** Not benchmark-run numbers: the scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty.

| Per solver step, 64 / 384, MacCormack dye                                                 | ms                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| solver alone                                                                              | about 2.0                               |
| solver with the pigment layer (diffusion in 1 or 2 sub-steps, drift, settling, flow drag) | about 2.2 (the layer adds 0.1 to 0.7)   |
| the same, semi-Lagrangian dye                                                             | about 1.2 (solver alone about 1.0)      |
| whole frame, wet-on-wet, one step per frame                                               | about 2.0 to 2.8                        |
| whole frame, ink drop in water (viscosity, vorticity)                                     | about 2.2                               |
| whole frame, sumi-e (3 diffusion sub-steps)                                               | about 2.8                               |
| `ink-wash` (non-simulated), the same canvas                                               | about 0.5 (below the measurement floor) |

What dominates: **the solver's dye advection** (MacCormack is two dispatches; switching the `Pigment advection` parameter to semi-Lagrangian takes about half off the whole step, at the price of softer ink), then the pressure solve and viscosity (8 Jacobi iterations cost about 0.35 ms), then the pigment layer, which is one dispatch at the dye resolution (about 40 texture loads per texel, most of them cached taps of the 3 x 3 wetness neighbourhood for the drift, and 3 stores) plus one more per diffusion sub-step. Diffusion is the part that scales with a parameter: cost steps up by one cheap dispatch per `0.12 / (dt H^2)` of diffusivity, up to `Diffusion sub-steps at most`. The brush's water pass runs only on frames with strokes. The velocity-drag pass was not timed on its own (one dispatch over the velocity grid, which is small).

Memory (HUD, 1662 x 932, defaults): about 14.7 MB in 20 textures: the solver's dye (3 x 2.1 MB at 683 x 384 x 8 B), the pigment layer's stain (2 x 2.1 MB), wetness (2 x 1.05 MB), the paper tile (0.26 MB) and the solver's velocity, pressure and fields; two more scratch textures (4.2 MB) appear when a preset needs more than one diffusion sub-step. The grain of the paper is tied to the dye resolution (one tile texel per dye texel), so raising `Pigment texels` makes the grain finer relative to the canvas.

Verdict: **Tier B**, about 2 to 3 ms per frame here at 1662 x 932 against a 6 ms budget, a third to half of it; it is compute (the scorecard says so: no WebGL2 fallback without rewriting the solver and the layer as render passes over float targets). Not measured: any integrated GPU other than Apple's, any phone, any browser but Chromium; `mobile` stays `untested`.

## Pitfalls

Each of these was hit while building it.

- **A saturating drift turns any ripple into stripes.** The first version drifted pigment toward drier paper at full speed for any gradient however small. A stroke laid frame by frame has a ripple of a percent or two in its wetness, and every ripple became a bar across the stroke, one per frame of the drag. Two changes fixed it: the drift has a dead zone (it starts at 0.3 of its reference gradient), and the point splats the stirrer drips while a pointer is nearly still no longer carry water (a full blob of water each frame scalloped the film's edge).
- **Per-axis drift draws diamonds.** With the drift taken from the sign of the difference along each axis alone, every round drop grew a cross and a diamond; the gradient at a face must use its tangential component as well, so the speed depends on the size of the whole gradient.
- **16-bit stores lose mass.** The solver's dye is `rgba16float`, and on the development machine the store rounds toward zero: a spreading point lost 0.03 percent of its mass per step (3 percent over 80 steps at 60 Hz, 11 percent over 320 steps at 240 Hz). So the conservation tests run the kernels on a 32-bit variant of the same shader (`format: 'rgba32float'`, the text with the format swapped), where they hold to 1e-3, and one test runs the real format and asserts the loss is bounded and that no mass is ever created. The loss is also the reason a stain is a separate layer: an untouched stain texel is bit for bit the same step after step (a test checks it), whereas suspended pigment that keeps changing slowly fades by rounding. Other GPUs may round to nearest; not measured.
- **Tiny additions vanish in half floats.** Adding 1e-4 to a stain of 2 does nothing at 11 bits of mantissa. A residue under 1.5e-3 therefore settles in one go instead of hanging about for ever.
- **Momentum ripples the ink.** The brush's push advects what the last frame laid before the next frame's capsule lands, so the ramps no longer add up to a uniform stroke: a ripple along the stroke in proportion to `Push` (measured as the relative standard deviation of the ink along the stroke, 16 texels off its axis: 0.8 percent with no push, 8 percent at a push of 0.1, 31 percent at 0.4). The watercolour presets use almost none (0.05 to 0.1); ink in water needs it and shows it as the curl of the plume. The same ripple is in `fluid-dye`.
- **The explicit scheme caps the diffusivity.** At 384 texels one pass can only diffuse 5e-5 heights squared per second (`0.12 / (dt H^2)`): a 4 percent of the canvas bloom in 5 seconds needs about 3 sub-steps. It is a cost, not a bug; the parameter's range stops at 3e-4.
- **Paper that is dry does not flow.** Without the drag pass the push of a stroke carried the ink across dry paper like water on glass; with it, flow lives only where the film is. The drag also means a drop on dry paper does not swirl.
- **A circle is not a drop.** The first blooms were perfect circles with a ring and a core, like targets. The water the brush adds is scaled by two octaves of noise fixed to the sheet, so the wet front, and with it the rim, is ragged where the paper takes water unevenly.
- **Dark paper cannot be subtractive.** Pigment cannot darken a dark sheet. The dark preset reads density as opacity of a glowing hue; at first it drew a thin coloured contour round every wash (the faint haze has no stable hue), hence the toe over the faintest densities.
- **Resizing clears the sheet.** The grids are re-created when the canvas aspect changes by more than 6 percent (`fluid-dye` does the same); in between the picture is stretched. Changing `Velocity cells` or `Pigment texels` clears it.
- **One parameter set per submitted command buffer.** The pigment layer's parameters go through one uniform buffer written with `queue.writeBuffer`; two frames encoded before one submit share the last values. The water pass has its own uniform buffer for this reason.
- **Measuring on a shared machine.** Timings moved by a factor of up to ten between runs while other work used the GPU; quote minima of many runs and slopes, not single readings.

What the model does not do, candidly. **Back-runs** are only the lighter band and darker edges of a lifted stain; the cauliflower fronts of a real back-run (fractal edges as the new water pushes into a half-dry wash) are not reproduced. **Pigments** all share one absorption, granulation and lifting coefficient; real staining and granulating pigments differ. **Fibres** modulate diffusion isotropically; they do not steer it along their direction. **Brush texture** (dry-brush streaks, bristle marks) is absent; the dry brush is a low `Water on the brush` and a high absorption. The wetness is not carried by the flow. There is no transparent-paper mode (the sheet is opaque; the page behind shows only in contexts that dim or frame the canvas) and no obstacles. The pigment layer ignores the solver's solid mask and boundary mode (the sheet is a closed box).

## When to use it

An interactive set piece where the visitor paints pigment onto paper and watches it bloom, feather, mix and dry; or an ambient sheet where drops land and spread on their own. Tier B: about 2 to 3 ms per frame on Apple Silicon at a 1662 x 932 canvas with the default grids, a hero or a dedicated section, not decoration behind every page. The `hero` context shows it behind a card; the `backdrop` context dims it to a ghost, which suits sumi-e better than the colourful presets.

**Against the non-simulated `ink-wash`.** What the simulation buys: the picture is made by the visitor (strokes where they painted, colours mixing subtractively where they cross, ink that stays when the water stops, a darker rim that is the result of the water drying rather than a threshold of a noise field, back-runs when water meets a half-dry wash), and it behaves like pigment under changed parameters (absorption, drying, diffusion) instead of showing a different noise. What it costs: about four to five times the GPU time of `ink-wash` (2 to 3 ms against about 0.5, which is below the floor of the measurement), compute shaders (no WebGL2 fallback), 15 MB of textures instead of a fraction of one, state that must survive resize and device loss (the sheet is cleared by both), a first frame that needs seeding, no deterministic screenshot, and about 38 parameters instead of 16, many of which interact. When the fake is the better choice: a calm background behind long text (it never competes with it and has no cost spikes); anywhere without compute or on a phone; for reduced motion; whenever nobody paints on it, since the look of drying paint at rest is what `ink-wash` already gives for a fifth of the price; for a static page where a pre-rendered tile is cheaper still. Use this when the painting is the point.

If it is too heavy: switch `Pigment advection` to semi-Lagrangian (softer ink, about half off the step), lower `Pigment texels` to 256, keep `Diffusion sub-steps at most` at 1, or fall back to `ink-wash`.
