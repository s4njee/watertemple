## How it works

A particle fluid is a cloud of points; drawn as spheres it looks like a bag of marbles. Screen-space fluid rendering (van der Laan, Green and Sainz 2009) turns it into a liquid skin using only what the camera sees, so it needs nothing but the particle positions and costs nothing like a surface mesh. This effect is the renderer (`src/gpu/fluid-surface/`, API in `index.ts`) plus a demo that drives it with three synthetic sources, so the surface can be judged before any solver exists. A solver hands over a `ParticleSet` (`src/gpu/particles/types.ts`) and gets a liquid.

Six passes per frame, each labelled for the HUD:

1. **`fluid depth`** (`sphere-depth.wgsl`). Every particle is a camera-facing quad, four vertices from an instance-step vertex buffer, shaded as the sphere it stands for: the fragment intersects its pixel's ray with the sphere, discards the corners of the quad, and writes the exact surface depth with `frag_depth` (so spheres intersect correctly and the nearest wins) and the linear view-space depth to an `r32float` image. Fragments behind the scene's own depth buffer are discarded, so a particle hidden by a wall never reaches the filter. The chain runs at `scale` of the output (half by default).
2. **`fluid smooth`** (`smooth.wgsl`). A separable filter, `iterations` rounds of horizontal then vertical, melts the spheres into one surface. The kernel radius follows the projected size of a particle at the pixel's depth (`filterRadius` particle radii, capped at `maxFilterRadius` pixels), so the look is the same near and far. Two filters, compared below.
3. **`fluid normals`** (`normals.wgsl`). View-space position is rebuilt from depth; the normal is the cross product of the differences to the neighbours, and in each direction the one-sided difference with the smaller depth change wins (or the only valid one), so a pixel on a silhouette never takes its normal from across the gap.
4. **`fluid thickness`** and **`fluid blur`** (`thickness.wgsl`, `thickness-blur.wgsl`). Each particle adds the length its sphere cuts out of the pixel's ray, additively and with no depth test, into an `rg16float` image at a quarter of the output size (`thicknessScale`), clipped where the scene hides the rest of the sphere, then a Gaussian blur. The green channel carries thickness times the particle's scalar `w`, so the ratio of the two is a smooth mean of `w` along the ray: the foam hook.
5. **`fluid composite`** (`composite.wgsl`), at full resolution. The surface is rebuilt by interpolating the chain with weights that skip background texels, which gives a half-resolution chain a clean anti-aliased silhouette. The scene colour is looked up through an offset taken from the normal and scaled by the thickness (dropped where it would pull in something in front of the liquid), tinted by Beer-Lambert absorption over the thickness (`absorption.wgsl`, the named water types or a custom colour), and a Fresnel-weighted sky reflection and a GGX sun highlight are added (`fresnel.wgsl`, `sky.wgsl`, `specular.wgsl`). The liquid is hidden by scene geometry in front of it by comparing with the scene depth, with a short fade so the cut with a floor is not a stair. The shared geometry and filter helpers are in `src/wgsl/fluid-surface.wgsl`.

The demo's particles come from a compute pass (`sources.wgsl`): each particle is a pure function of its index and the time, spread by an integer R3 sequence with a jitter of one spacing, so a million particles cost no CPU. The **ball** wobbles, reaches a neck towards its twin, splits and merges every nine seconds; the **wave** is a slab of liquid carrying waves and a swell; the **stream** pours into a pool, thins as it speeds up, breaks into drops, and splashes. The `view` control swaps the finished surface for the spheres it replaces (`spheres`, `split`) or any intermediate (`raw-depth`, `smoothed-depth` with depth contours, `normals`, `thickness`); `compare` renders the bilateral filter on the left and the narrow-range filter on the right in any of them.

### Bilateral against narrow-range

The bilateral filter weights a neighbour by `exp(-dz^2 / 2 sigma^2)` of its depth difference, so a depth far from the centre's is dropped. The narrow-range filter (Truong and Yuksel 2018) keeps every neighbour but clamps its depth to the centre's depth plus or minus a window (`rangeScale` radii), so a foreign surface can move the result by at most the window and a slanted surface still averages all of its neighbours. This is the clamped form described in the paper; the paper's further steps (an estimate of the filtered depth to centre the window on) are not implemented. Background pixels are skipped by both, so an isolated droplet keeps its sphere and a silhouette is not pulled outwards.

What it looked like (the `normals` view with `compare` on, ball source, `filterRadius` 2.5, 3 iterations, full-resolution chain, about 3 particle radii of smoothing): the bilateral surface comes out terraced. On every steep part of the ball the normals break into rectangular patches, with horizontal runs where the surface is seen from above and vertical ones where it faces sideways. That is the separable bilateral artefact: on a slanted surface a neighbour a few pixels away is already further than the range sigma, so the kernel shrinks to a sliver along the axis it is filtering, each row or column settles on its own value, and the second pass cannot reconcile them. The narrow-range surface in the same frame is one smooth gradient with no patches. Silhouettes are the same for both (both skip background); at a depth step as large as the one between two blobs at different depths neither moves the far side (tests/fluid-surface.test.ts: narrow-range moves nothing by more than its window, bilateral by under 0.05 world units). Recommendation: narrow-range, always; it costs the same (the tap loop is identical, one clamp instead of one `exp`) and removes the terraces. Keep the bilateral one only as the baseline to compare against.

## Cost

The current default at 1080p is also measured in `docs/particle-scale-study.md`, with raw samples and exact parameters in `docs/particle-scale-study.json`. The earlier isolated renderer comparisons below use their own stated configurations. A captured poster is the no-WebGPU fallback.

**Method.** Measured headlessly through Dawn's Node bindings on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal), a 1920 x 1080 `rgba16float` target, `scale` 1/2, `thicknessScale` 1/4, narrow-range, 3 iterations, filter radius 2.5, unless a row says otherwise. Each figure is the slope of "N renders in one submit against wall-clock time" (N between about 3 and 60, so the roughly 1 ms floor of a submit drops out), best of two or three rounds; each stage was measured alone by repeating only that stage (`stages` option of `render`) after one full render had filled its inputs. They are indicative: the machine was shared with other test runs, which moved single rows by up to a factor of two (the tables below keep the best figure seen for a row), and nothing here was run on an integrated GPU or a phone. The HUD agrees where it can be compared: the default view at 968 x 976 and 60k particles read 3.6 ms of frame GPU time at 120 fps on a quiet machine and 6.7 to 8 ms on a busy one; a million-particle wave read about 43 ms. Per-pass HUD figures overlap on this GPU and must not be added up.

Read the ranges as noise and the ratios as the result. The smoothing chain: full resolution is about three times the smoothing cost and one and a half times the depth pass of half resolution; quarter resolution is nearly free but makes the silhouette chunky. Each iteration is a constant cost of about 0.3 ms at this coverage (a horizontal and a vertical pass of up to 33 taps), so the iteration count is a straight dial. The two filters cost the same: the loop is identical, one `exp` against one `clamp` (the rows above differ by less than the noise). The thickness resolution barely matters, because the pass is bound by particles, not pixels: use `thicknessStride` to make it cheaper, not `thicknessScale`.

**What fits.** Tier C (12 ms at 1080p, Apple Silicon): the default views (60k to 200k particles) cost 3 to 5 ms with a simulation not included, and the renderer alone stays under 12 ms up to about 400 000 small particles; a million costs 22 ms (14 ms with `thicknessStride` 4) and does not fit, nor does a camera so close that particles are over about 8 pixels in radius. A solver shares the Tier C budget with the renderer, so at 100k particles it has about 8 ms left. **Tier B cut-down:** `scale` 1/4, `thicknessScale` 1/8, `thicknessStride` 2, 1 iteration, `filterRadius` 1.25 and 40 000 particles (the `cheap cut` preset) measured 1.7 ms on the wave at 30% coverage on this laptop, which would leave the 6 ms integrated-GPU budget a large margin if an integrated GPU scales like this one, which is not known. Keep the radius at or below about 3 pixels in the chain; the bigger the particles, the more it costs, whatever else is cut.

**Memory** at 1080p with `scale` 1/2: three `r32float` images and a `depth32float` buffer at 960 x 540 (8.3 MB), two `rgba16float` normal images (8.3 MB), two small `rg16float` thickness images (1 MB): 17.6 MB, scaling with the pixel count of the chain. The particle buffer is 16 bytes per particle (16 MB at the million-particle capacity of the demo). Bundle size: not measured yet.

## Pitfalls

- **It is a screen-space trick.** Everything is derived from one depth image, so there is no fluid behind fluid: where two sheets overlap on screen only the nearer one has a surface, and the one behind it exists only as thickness (it darkens the near one, it is not seen through it). Pour a stream in front of a wave and the stream's silhouette shows, the wave behind it does not refract through it.
- **One refraction layer, and thickness is approximate.** The background is looked up once, through an offset from the front surface's normal times the total thickness; there is no second bend where the ray leaves the liquid. Thickness is a sum of sphere chords, so it counts overlapping spheres twice: the `thicknessGain` (the volume a particle stands for over the volume of its sphere) fixes the average, not the local value. The demo sets it from the source's volume; a solver should pass `mass / restDensity / (4/3 pi r^3)`. Absorption and refraction both depend on it, so a wrong gain shows as water that is too dark or too clear, never as a visible seam.
- **Temporal shimmer.** The surface is rebuilt every frame from point samples of a particle cloud, so a particle that moves a pixel changes the depth under it. Orbiting a still cloud makes the highlights crawl a little, and a cloud of fewer particles per pixel crawls more. The filter radius is the knob (more smoothing, less crawl, rounder detail); there is no temporal filter here, and none is cheap because a moving camera invalidates the history.
- **The silhouette inherits the spheres.** Smoothing skips background pixels so droplets survive and outlines stay put, which means the outline is the union of the spheres, scalloped at the scale of one particle radius. At half resolution the composite blurs this into a soft one-to-two pixel edge; at full resolution it is a visibly beaded rim on a close-up ball. Thin features are limited by the particle radius: a stream thinner than about two radii is a row of beads.
- **Hidden particles cost as much as visible ones.** Every particle is drawn whether or not anything can see it, and writing `frag_depth` turns early depth rejection off, so the inside of a deep pool is rasterised in full. See the cost tables: this, not the filter, is what a larger fluid or a closer camera pays for.
- **Filter radius is capped.** Past `maxFilterRadius` pixels (16 by default) the kernel skips texels, which on raw depth leaves a parity texture (each pixel class averages different texels). The cap is why a camera very close to big particles smooths less than the same scene far away; raise `scale` down or the cap up, not both.
- **Depth precision.** The chain is `r32float`; half floats cannot carry normals. 32-bit floats are not filterable without `float32-filterable` and not blendable without `float32-blendable`, so the chain is read with `textureLoad` and the composite interpolates by hand. Thickness is `rg16float`, which is filterable and blendable everywhere. Near-rim pixels of a sphere are ill-conditioned (the depth slope is vertical there): the intersection is written in the perpendicular-offset form, which fixed errors of a thousandth of a unit at the rim.
- **No reflection of the scene.** The reflection is the sky function only, which is why the container, the floor and the posts never appear in the liquid's reflection, only refracted through it. A cube map or a planar capture would cost another pass and a bind group; the renderer has the hook (the composite takes the sun direction and haze, nothing else) but not the texture.
- **One `render` per submit.** Uniforms are written with `queue.writeBuffer`, so two renders of one `FluidSurface` before a single submit both see the last one's values. Use one renderer per view.
- **Needs core WebGPU only.** No optional feature is required (`timestamp-query` is the HUD's). It does need storage-free vertex input only, so it also works where vertex-stage storage buffers are restricted; the particle sources in the demo are compute and are the only part that does not run on a compat adapter.

## When to use it

On a dedicated page where a liquid is the subject: a drink or a pour in a product page, a tank, a toy, a game level, a landing experience with a pool the visitor stirs. It earns its cost when the liquid is the thing on screen and the scene around it is simple enough to refract: the floor pattern and the posts in this demo are what make the surface legible, and a plain background would hide most of the work. It serves any particle solver in Epic 7 (SPH, position-based fluids, MLS-MPM) by taking a `ParticleSet`, and other liquids by changing the material: the `thick syrup` preset is the same renderer with an amber absorption, a higher index of refraction, a wider filter and more iterations.

Tier C as a story, because the solvers it serves are. The renderer alone is cheap enough for a Tier B feature at modest counts (see the cut-down in Cost). It is not a background or a card: it takes the whole page's frame budget on a laptop. Where it does not fit: ambient decoration (use `shallow-water` or `ripples`), a small blob (use a ray-marched SDF blob, Epic 9), and a view where you must see a liquid through a liquid or through a curved glass (use ray marching or a ray-traced volume, Epic 6.9). There is no WebGL2 version in this repository; the renderer itself is render passes only, but the solvers are compute.
