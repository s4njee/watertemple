## How it works

A tank of water simulated with the moving-least-squares material point method (MLS-MPM, Hu et al. 2018), specialised to a weakly compressible fluid. Particles carry the fluid (position, velocity, an affine velocity matrix C, a density ratio); a regular grid in the tank does the physics; the two exchange information every step with the quadratic B-spline, so each particle touches 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 grid nodes. There is **no neighbour search**: where SPH and position-based fluids find, for every particle, the particles within a radius, here two particles "see" each other because they write to the same nodes. The solver is `src/gpu/sim/mpm.ts` with `mpm-core.wgsl` (the shared maths), `mpm-clear.wgsl`, `mpm-p2g1.wgsl`, `mpm-p2g2.wgsl`, `mpm-grid.wgsl`, `mpm-g2p.wgsl` and the sorted variants `mpm-p2g1s.wgsl`, `mpm-density.wgsl`, `mpm-p2g2s.wgsl`; this page is the demo that drives it (`index.ts`, `scene.wgsl`) and hands the particles to `createFluidSurface` for drawing.

**One step, five passes** (every pass is labelled for the HUD; all state is storage buffers, 8 per stage allowed, at most 7 used):

1. **Clear** (`mpm-clear.wgsl`). Zero the grid: per node four `i32`, mass and the three momentum components.
2. **P2G, pass 1** (`mpm-p2g1.wgsl`). Every particle adds its mass and its momentum, including the APIC affine term, to its 27 nodes: `m_i = sum w_ip`, `q_i = sum w_ip (v_p + C_p (x_i - x_p))`. Many particles add to the same node at once, so the sums are atomic adds on **fixed-point `i32`** (below).
3. **P2G, pass 2, the fluid stress** (`mpm-p2g2.wgsl`). Each particle gathers the mass around it back from the grid with the same weights, which is its **density**, `r = rho / rho_0 = (sum w_ip m_i) / (particles per cell at rest)`. The pressure comes from a Tait equation of state, `p / rho_0 = (c^2 / gamma)(r^gamma - 1)`, whose sound speed for small compressions is exactly `c` (the stiffness knob); negative pressure is clamped to zero (a `tension` parameter lets a fraction of it through). A viscous stress `2 nu D` from the symmetric part of C is added. The momentum change `w (4 / h^2) dt (V p I - 2 V mu D)(x_i - x_p)` of the MLS-MPM stress term is scattered to the nodes, again with fixed-point atomics. With unit particle mass and `V = V_0 / r` this needs no absolute density: `V p = (p / rho_0) / r`.
4. **Grid update** (`mpm-grid.wgsl`). Decode the sums, `v_i = q_i / m_i` (both are fixed point with the same scale, so it cancels), damp, add gravity, then the boundaries: the six faces of the container (which **move**: each has a velocity, and a node on or beyond a face has its normal velocity held at or above the wall's, its tangential velocity blended towards the wall's by `wallFriction`) and up to eight colliders (spheres, boxes, planes, with a velocity and a friction). The speed is limited to `maxSpeed` and anything non-finite is zeroed.
5. **G2P** (`mpm-g2p.wgsl`). Every particle gathers its new velocity `v_p = sum w v_i` and its new affine matrix `C_p = (4 / h^2) sum w v_i (x_i - x_p)^T`, moves by `dt v_p`, is pushed out of the colliders and clamped inside the (moving) walls, and gets its foam value.

**Fixed point.** WGSL atomics are integers only, so mass and momentum are summed as `i32` with `MPM_FIXED_SCALE` = 2^20 units per 1.0 (`mpm-core.wgsl`). One unit of mass is one **particle** (every particle has mass 1; the equation of state is written in ratios, so the physical rest density never appears). A node holds up to +-2047.99 of mass or of mass times velocity. That is 255 particles per cell at the default 8 per cell (32 times the rest density), or 100 particles moving at 20 m/s. The resolution is 9.5e-7 per unit: a lone particle at 1e-3 m/s deposits 4.2e-4 of momentum on its central node and is returned to within 0.5 % (tested), one at 1e-4 m/s to 3 %, one at 1e-5 m/s to about 11 %. Quantisation of the mass is tested too: the grid total equals the particle count within 1e-5 of one particle per particle. Beyond the range an `i32` sum wraps; the grid pass treats a wrapped (negative) mass as an empty node and clamps every velocity it decodes, so a clump the format cannot hold gives a wrong but bounded velocity and never a NaN (tested with 5000 particles on one node). Pressure impulses are bounded the same way: the density ratio the pressure sees is capped at `densityLimit` (1.8), so one particle's impulse stays inside the range.

**Stability.** The explicit scheme needs `dt <= courant h / (c + v_max)`. Information moves at most the fluid's own speed plus a pressure wave's per unit time, and a step can only be right if neither crosses more than a fraction of a cell, because the quadratic stencil couples a particle to nodes within 1.5 h. `v_max` is the limit the solver itself enforces on nodes and particles (`maxSpeed`), so the bound holds in every state it can reach, and a particle moves at most `v_max dt < courant h` per step. The viscous term is a diffusion and needs `nu dt / h^2` below a constant; `nu` is clamped to `0.1 h^2 / dt`. The effect asks for `substeps` steps per 1/60 s; if the stiffness is too high for that step the **stiffness is reduced** (`effective.soundSpeed`, tested), and if even the softest stiffness does not fit the step is shortened. Time advances in fixed steps (`createFixedStepper`, at most `maxSteps` per frame, the backlog is dropped), so 60 and 120 Hz give the same fluid (tested). Measured limit (dam break, 49 920 particles, 48 x 32 x 32 cells, c = 12, v_max = 10): `courant` 0.5 to 2 give the same flow; 4 and above explode (the speed limit saturates and the fluid never settles). The default is 1, a factor of about three below the limit; smaller steps lower the **rest noise** (the kinetic energy of a pool that should be still falls from 1e-2 J/kg at 1 to 1e-4 at 0.1), which is the reason to spend sub-steps and not safety.

**The density estimate at a wall.** Gathering density from the grid reads low near a wall (the nodes beyond it hold no mass) and would let the fluid pile against the wall and sink a pool by 10 to 20 %. A fluid at exactly rest density filling the container leaves `rest_ppc * F_i` on node i, where `F_i` is the share of the node's kernel inside the walls (a product of three cubic polynomials, `mpm_kernel_above`). Dividing the gathered density by the same share seen from the particle (`wallCompensation`, default on) removes the deficit exactly for a flat wall and costs nine polynomial evaluations; away from the walls it is 1. The pool test shows the difference (below, Pitfalls). The free surface is left alone: its deficit is real and gives the zero-pressure boundary.

**Sorted P2G.** The two scatter passes are bound by atomics: 100 000 particles issue 10 million atomic adds per pass, about 4 ms per step on this machine as one thread per particle. `sorted: true` (the default) bins the particles by the cell their stencil starts in (floor(x / h - 0.5), with the framework's counting sort, which also reorders the particle data so each cell's particles are contiguous) and lets one thread own one row of one cell: it adds the contributions of all of that cell's particles to its three nodes in registers and writes one atomic add per value. About 8 particles per cell means about 8 times fewer atomics, and neighbouring threads work on neighbouring cells so they write different addresses at any moment. The sort is an accelerator, not a neighbour search (nothing reads another particle), and the unsorted path is kept as the reference and is what the tests compare against. Particles the sort puts into the wrong cell (it pads the cell by 1/4096) are handled by a slow path, so the result is the same grid (tested to a few units in the last place).

**Foam.** `positions.w` is a 0..1 scalar the renderer reads as foam: `1 - smoothstep(0.2, 0.45, r)` for a particle whose density has fallen to spray level (isolated particles in a splash read 0.05 to 0.2), or `smoothstep(3, 7, speed)` for a fast one, whichever is larger, fading at 1.5 per second once the cause is gone. It stays in 0..1 (tested through a splash). The same particle buffers feed the three debug views: foam (spheres), speed, and the density ratio.

**The demo.** Three scenes, each restartable (the `restart` toggle, or a change of scene, count or grid): a **wave tank** (a wall, the piston, pushes in and out with a 1.9 s period and sends a wave down 3.2 m of water 0.55 m deep to break on a 24 degree beach, a plane collider drawn as a wedge); a **dam break** (a 0.9 m column released around two pillars and a low wall: box colliders); a **pour** (a stream of 8.5 cm radius into a pool, emitted layer by layer from the CPU at the lattice spacing). The count sets the particles per cell (the water's volume is fixed in metres): 100 000 particles in the wave tank is about 7 per cell on the default 64-cell grid, and the count and the grid are separate knobs. Dragging empty space orbits; a press whose ray enters the tank starts a push, and dragging then moves a solid sphere (a collider with the pointer's velocity) along the plane at the water level; a tap drops a blob of 300 particles. The camera drifts when left alone and the wave maker runs, so the page is alive untouched. The scene (floor tiles, tank outline, beach, pillars, sky) is drawn into colour and depth with `src/gpu/scene`, the liquid by `createFluidSurface` (narrow-range filter), then tonemapped; the other views draw the particles as opaque sphere impostors over the tonemapped scene.

## Cost

The maintained study in `docs/particle-scale-study.md` measures complete 1080p frames at 10,000, 40,000 and 100,000 requested particles, including this scene and the surface chain. Raw samples, the exact parameters and the distinction between a timestamp span and a repeated-workload slope are recorded in `docs/particle-scale-study.json`. The 100,000-particle default is within the 12 ms Tier C target by the workload slope in this M1 Max run; there is no mobile or other-device verdict. ## Pitfalls

What MLS-MPM gets wrong for water, and what was done about each (the numbers are from `tests/mpm.test.ts` and from scratch runs of the same solver; "pool" is 16 384 particles in a 0.8 x 0.8 x 0.4 m block on a 32 cube grid of 5 cm cells):

- **Numerical viscosity and dissipation.** The particle-to-grid-to-particle round trip is a low-pass filter applied once per step, so a splash loses energy at a rate that grows with the number of steps and not with physics: a dam break's mean kinetic energy falls from 3.1 J/kg at 0.5 s to 1.2 at 1 s, 0.43 at 3 s and 0.24 at 4 s (49 920 particles, 5 cm cells), and a wave that should slosh for many seconds fades in two or three. APIC (the C matrix) is what keeps angular momentum, and the pool test shows it is not enough to keep a wave alive for long. Nothing here adds energy back, and a physical viscosity is clamped to what the step can diffuse (`nu <= 0.1 h^2 / dt`, 0.07 m^2/s at 5 cm and 3 ms), so the fluid is always more viscous than water at this resolution and cell size. The demo keeps `damping` at 0.5 per second, which only calms what the filter does not.
- **A pool never quite rests.** After an impulsive start (gravity switched on at rest density) the weakly compressible fluid rings with sound waves that the equation of state does not damp: a pool at rest keeps a kinetic energy of 0.001 to 0.01 J/kg (an rms speed of 0.05 to 0.15 m/s, about a pixel a second in this view) for the 15 seconds measured, falling by roughly 100 times when the step is five times smaller (courant 0.1 against 1) and by 4 with `damping` 0.5 per second. A real viscosity at its clamp kills it (50 times lower) but also the splash. A bulk-viscosity pressure term `-zeta div(v)` was tried and removed: it changed nothing measurable (the ringing is not in the divergence of C). The rendered surface smooths the jitter; the honest summary is "calm, not still".
- **Compressibility.** Pressure is `c^2 (r - 1)` for small compressions, so a column of depth D compresses by about `g D / c^2` at the bottom: 2.7 % for c = 12 m/s and 0.4 m, 5.6 % for the defaults here (c = 10, 0.55 m); the pool test pins the settled depth within 4 % of the initial one (2.1 % measured at c = 12). Raising c costs steps in proportion (dt ~ 1 / (c + v_max)). The pressure the equation of state may use is capped (`densityLimit`, r <= 1.8) so one impact cannot overflow the fixed-point format; beyond that cap the fluid compresses further without resistance.
- **The free surface and the walls read low.** The density gathered from the grid is the mass within 1.5 cells, so the top cell of a pool reads 0.74 of rest density (measured: 0.735, then 0.986 one cell lower) and a particle at a flat wall about the same. At the free surface that is right (zero pressure there is the boundary condition). At a wall it let the fluid pile up and sink: **a pool without `wallCompensation` sinks 11 to 17 % of its depth, with it 2 %** (the test compares the two). The correction is exact for a flat wall (see "How it works") and does nothing for a collider: fluid compresses a little against the pillars and the beach, and the beach shows it as a slightly denser, darker sheet of water where the wave runs up.
- **Clumping, voids, grid-aligned noise.** The pressure only sees density smoothed over about three cells, so structure below that scale is not resisted: after a few seconds a pool that started on a perfect lattice (8 particles per cell, spacing half a cell) has 4 to 12 particles per cell (5th to 95th percentile) with a mean of 8, no repulsion at the particle scale, and the negative-pressure clamp lets a void stay a void. The initial lattice is jittered by 15 % of the spacing (a perfect lattice, aligned with the grid, was no calmer and looks like a crystal in the sphere view) and fewer than about 4 particles per cell is visibly noisy; more is smoother and cheaper per particle (see Cost). No strings or sheets along the grid axes were seen in the liquid view; the sphere view shows the cell-scale granularity. `tension` (negative pressure) was not needed and is off.
- **Volume drift.** Mass is exact (the particle count is fixed and the tests check the grid total after every few hundred steps against it within 1e-5 per particle), and the density-from-grid pressure has no drift of its own: a pool's settled depth after 15 s is the one after 3 s to within the noise (0.4797 m of top surface against 0.4817 at the same time-step). What shifts is the apparent volume: spray and the compressed surface layer make the 90th percentile of a settled dam break 0 to 20 % above the level that `N V_0 / area` gives (the test allows 20 %).
- **Fixed-point range.** A node holds +-2047.99 units; a clump of 5000 particles on one node would overflow (the failure is a bounded wrong velocity, tested). At 8 per cell nothing like that can happen; compressing a pool to 32 times the rest density would. Slow particles are quantised: below 1e-5 m/s a lone particle's momentum disappears into the last bit.
- **Atomics and non-determinism.** The sums are integer, so they are exact and order-independent in the unsorted path; the sorted path adds floats in registers in an order that varies from run to run (the sort's ranks come from atomics), so two runs differ in the last bits of the grid and then, like any chaotic flow, in the splash. Particle indices change every step (the sort reorders the data): keep per-particle state in the system's attributes.
- **Moving walls.** A wall moves at most `wallCourant` (0.4) cells per step, so a faster request is slowed rather than tunnelled through (tested; no particle ever left the box). A wall that retreats leaves a gap the fluid does not follow instantly (the piston's return stroke): that is the physics, not a bug.
- **One submit holds 2048 steps.** Per-step uniforms live in a ring, so two updates in one submit that together exceed it would overwrite each other; `step` and `update` throw at once for a single call over the ring and wrap quietly beyond (the demo does one update per submit).
- **The renderer's limits apply.** One refraction layer, depth from the sphere impostors, droplets as single spheres: a lone spray particle is a visible bead, and foam is a colour mix, not bubbles. Keep the particle radius under about 5 chain pixels (the defaults: 0.75 of the spacing, 3 to 5 px at 1080p).
- **The first frame is slow to appear** in the preview browser (several seconds, mostly compiling about ten compute pipelines and the surface chain); not isolated further.
- **Phones.** Not tried. A frame is about 60 compute dispatches, four to six steps of ten, which is dispatch-latency territory on a mobile GPU even before the atomics; the effect is not offered there.

## When to use it

On a dedicated page where a body of water is the subject and the particle count matters more than the physics: a tank, a pour, a pool toy, a splash with 100 000 or more drops, on a discrete or Apple Silicon GPU. It earns its cost where SPH or position-based fluids would not reach the count: their cost per particle step grows with the neighbours it has to find and visit, this one's with the grid it has to clear and the atomics it issues, and the grid scales with the volume, not with the fluid. Tier C by the shape of the work; see Cost for what fits the 12 ms.

Where it does not fit: anything that must look incompressible and still (the pool rings, the surface shimmers faintly); fine detail below the cell size (there is none: the cell is 5 cm here, the particles half that, and a thin sheet is a row of beads); a small or cheap effect (the grid, the sort and the dispatches are a fixed price); phones and integrated GPUs, until measured; a fluid with a free surface that must hold its volume for minutes (use an Eulerian solver or position-based fluids). For a calm surface use `shallow-water` or `ripples` (Tier A and B); for a splash on a budget use the Tier B cut-down below or the `particles` effect's spray.
