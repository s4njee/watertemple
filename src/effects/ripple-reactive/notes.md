## How it works

The surface is the `ripples` solver (one heightfield, one shading pass) with the pointer demoted to a gentle extra and two other inputs driving it: the page's scroll and sound. Nothing about the shading changes; what is new is what is fed to `solver.addDrop`.

**Scroll** (`trackScroll` from `src/gpu/scroll.ts`, read once per frame; it follows inner scrollers as well as the window, so the `backdrop` context's article works). The smoothed scroll velocity, in CSS pixels per second, is divided by 1500 (that is "fast"; reading is a fifth of it or less) and clamped. It does three things. (1) Waves enter from the leading edge: scrolling down brings new content up from the bottom, so the bottom edge is leading, and drops land there at a rate that grows with speed (about 3 per second at reading speed, 12 per second at full speed), each small and slightly dented. (2) A light rain over the whole surface at a fraction of that rate, so the disturbance is not only a line at one edge. (3) A slosh on an abrupt stop: the detector remembers the recent peak speed (half a second of memory) and when the speed falls below a small value after a peak above 0.4, it heaps water against the edge the page was moving toward, digs a trough on the opposite side, and then the two rock back and forth and settle by damping. A cool-down of 0.7 seconds stops a stuttering scroll from sloshing repeatedly. When the canvas is off screen nothing is injected. The `scroll sensitivity` parameter scales all of it; with `prefers-reduced-motion` the effect runs at 40 percent. It is deliberately subtle: a page scrolled at reading speed looks like a page over water that has noticed you, and a hard flick makes a visible but soft swell, never a jolt.

**Audio** (`src/gpu/audio.ts`). An `AnalyserNode` (2048-point FFT) is read once per frame. Six log-spaced bands from 40 Hz to 12 kHz (edges at 40, 103, 268, 693, 1793, 4638 and 12000 Hz) are averaged from the byte spectrum, smoothed with a fast attack (15 ms) and a slower release (220 ms), then divided by each band's own remembered peak (auto-gain, peak released over 8 seconds, with a floor so silence and room noise are not blown up), so a quiet file and a loud one move the water equally. Onsets are detected on the bass group and on the high group separately: the energy minus a fast follower of itself must clear the larger of a fixed rise and 1.6 times the recent mean rise, the signal must still be rising, and an onset blocks the next for 0.14 seconds. A jump fires once, a plateau and a slow swell never, which the tests check on synthetic envelopes. The pure maths (edges, bin ranges, splitting, smoothing, gain, onsets, the built-in pattern) has no Web Audio in it and is tested without a browser.

The mapping, chosen to read musically: **bass** is broad and slow. A kick digs a wide dent near the middle that rebounds into a ring and pushes three drops up from the bottom edge, and sustained bass sends broad swells in from the left and right edges at a rate that follows the bass level squared. **Mids** are moderate rain (rate follows level to the power 1.5, so quiet passages are nearly still). **Highs** are fine, quick rain, and a high onset (a hat or clap) is a single sharp drop. Lower bands land lower in the pool and higher ones higher up. Every strength is clamped at 2.5 and every rate is bounded by a per-frame budget (16 impulses from audio, 8 from scroll, leaving room in the solver's 64), so the picture stays calm at any volume without leaning on the solver's own clamp. A status dot in the bottom-right corner shows the state: a hollow breathing ring while the audio is waiting for a click, a dot that swells with the level and flashes on onsets once it is listening, nothing if `indicator` is off.

**Sources.** The built-in source is a synthesised four-bar pattern at 104 BPM (kick, snare burst, hats, a bass line and a pentatonic arpeggio from oscillators and filtered noise), scheduled 0.3 seconds ahead of the audio clock from the frame loop, so there is no timer to leak and the music stops when the tab stops drawing. A dropped audio or video file (drag it on the canvas) replaces it: it plays from a local object URL through a media element, nothing leaves the machine, and the URL is revoked when it is replaced or the effect is destroyed. The microphone is never requested. Sound is routed to the analyser first and to the speakers through a gain that is 0 by default (`audible` fades it in), so analysing never needs audible output.

**The autoplay policy.** A browser keeps a new `AudioContext` suspended until the page has had a user gesture, and complains in the console if one is made earlier. So the context is not created at all until the first `pointerdown` or `pointerup` on the canvas, and a context that is still suspended after a gesture the browser did not count (a synthetic event) is resumed by the next real one. Until then the audio contributes nothing: the surface runs on scroll and its idle swell, which is the documented waiting state, and the status dot invites a click. The same helper is inert where there is no Web Audio at all.

Also: a seeded idle swell (a broad disc wandering on a slow path) and a little rain keep the surface alive with nothing else going on, and five drops are seeded on the first frame so a screenshot already shows moving water.

## Cost

Indicative HUD readings, Apple Silicon development laptop (Chromium, Metal, GPU shared with another tab), 1702 x 724 drawing buffer (1.2 Mpx, DPR 2), 256-cell grid: GPU about 0.85 ms per frame (shade about 0.5, solver passes about 0.3 each, overlapping), CPU about 0.3 ms per frame, with the synthesised beat running. Tier A on this machine. The audio side is small: one `getByteFrequencyData` of 1024 bins and some arithmetic per frame; the synthesiser creates about a dozen short-lived nodes per second. Not measured: integrated GPUs other than Apple's, phones, other browsers, or the CPU cost of the synth on a low-power device. `gpuMs` is left out of the scorecard for that reason.

Verified with numbers from the page, not by ear (the output cannot be heard from here): with the synthesised beat running, the low-band onsets landed 140 to 850 ms apart on the kick and bass notes and the high-band onsets 275 to 875 ms apart on the accented hats (12 of each in 6 seconds), band levels stayed in 0 to 1, and a generated 440 Hz sine file played through `useFile` put the most energy in the 268 to 693 Hz band.

## Pitfalls

- **Nothing happens before the first click, on purpose.** An AudioContext cannot start without a gesture. A page that wants sound from the first frame has to ask for a click (the status dot does) or accept that scroll drives the water until then.
- **A click that is not a gesture.** Pointer events dispatched from script are not trusted, so they do not start the context; use a real click when testing.
- **Hidden tabs stop the music.** The pattern is scheduled from the frame loop, so a tab that is not drawing is silent and resumes without a burst (a stall of more than 100 ms re-syncs the schedule).
- **Auto-gain needs time.** The remembered peak is released over 8 seconds, so after a loud passage a quiet one looks calm for a while before the gain catches up; the floor keeps a silent file from turning room noise into rain.
- **A browser may not decode the file.** `useFile` rejects when the element errors (an unsupported codec); the effect logs and keeps the built-in beat.
- **The scroll tracker also hears the window.** A canvas that is not in a scroller still reacts when the whole page scrolls, which is what the effect page itself does; a canvas that is off screen ignores scroll.
- **Slosh is a guess about feel.** The stop detector fires after a peak above 0.4 (about 600 px per second) drops below 0.06; slow, steady scrolling never triggers it. The amplitudes were tuned by eye in the `backdrop` and bare layouts, behind a veil that hides most of the water in `backdrop`; on a page with less covering it, lower `scroll sensitivity`.
- **A busy backdrop hides waves.** Pool tiles show every bend; sand and the test card hide small ones. Judge amplitude in the `height` view.
- **Resizing clears the water**, as in `ripples`.

## When to use it

A background that should answer the page rather than the pointer: a long article, a landing page, a music or podcast page. Tier A as measured here. Use `ripples` when the visitor's pointer is the input, and `ripple-dom` when the water should also respect the content over it. For a visualiser that must start without a click there is no way around the autoplay policy; analyse a recording the page already plays by routing its media element through the same helper (`useFile` does exactly that for a File). Without Web Audio the effect is a scroll-driven pond with an idle swell; without WebGPU show the backdrop image.
