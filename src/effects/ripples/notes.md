## How it works

A real wave equation, not a shader trick. The surface is a grid of cells (256 on the long side by default, square cells, the canvas's aspect ratio) whose state lives in textures between frames. Each simulation step is one pass over the grid: every cell reads its eight neighbours from the previous state, works out the laplacian, and writes its new height and velocity. The scheme is symplectic Euler, `v' = (v + k L(h)) decay`, `h' = h + v'`, the leapfrog scheme in disguise: no numerical damping of its own, so all the decay you see is the `damping` parameter. `L` is the isotropic 9-point laplacian `[1 4 1; 4 -20 4; 1 4 1] / 6`. The plain 5-point stencil is the textbook choice and it makes diamond-shaped rings, because waves travel slower on the diagonals; the 9-point one costs eight taps instead of four and the rings come out round (measured on a 128-cell grid, the ring along the diagonal is within 1% of its radius along an axis, and the front moves at the configured speed to within the 4% resolution of the measurement).

The solver is `src/gpu/sim/heightfield.ts`, in two interchangeable implementations of the same maths (`heightfield-core.wgsl` is shared, only the launch differs): **render**, three fullscreen fragment passes over ping-pong `rgba16float` render targets, and **compute**, the same three kernels as compute passes reading a texture and writing a write-only storage texture. Per frame: one _splat_ pass applies the queued impulses (up to 64: drops and moving-footprint displacements, in a uniform array), N _step_ passes advance time, and one _resolve_ pass writes the output texture the shading pass reads. The output is `(height, velocity, slope x, slope y)`: the slopes are a Sobel gradient of the height, computed once per frame rather than once per step, so shading needs one filtered tap and no neighbours (`src/wgsl/heightfield.wgsl`).

Stability and time. For the 9-point scheme the Courant number `c dt / cell` must stay below 0.866; the solver clamps it to 0.8. It does not pick a time step and hope: it derives the step from the wave speed, `dt = C cell / c`, so a faster wave or a finer grid costs more steps per second and never a larger step. The steps come from `createFixedStepper`, so the surface looks the same at 60 and 120 Hz (a test compares them) and a tab that wakes after a minute runs at most 16 steps and carries on (slow motion if the cap is hit, never instability). Walls: `reflect` mirrors the edge cell (zero normal slope), `absorb` adds a quadratic damping ramp over the last 14 cells so waves are eaten before they can reflect. An obstacle mask (one `r8unorm` texel per cell, 255 = solid) makes solid cells walls too; `solver.obstacles.setRects` rasterises rounded rectangles for the DOM-obstacle story.

Input is `trackPointers` (`src/gpu/pointer.ts`): every mouse, pen and finger with its path since the last frame. `createStirrer` (`src/gpu/sim/stir.ts`) turns that into water: a tap is one drop; a drag is a train of dents spaced a fraction of a radius apart along the interpolated path, strength rising with speed, with the spacing carried across frames so a fast swipe is as continuous as a slow one. Hover is the same trail at a quarter of the strength. By default nothing captures the pointer, so a finger that starts scrolling the page is cancelled by the browser and a full-width canvas never traps a scroll; the `touch drags` parameter switches to `touch-action: none` for a "stir to play" surface.

Ambient disturbance (`index.ts`): a seeded Poisson process of drops at `rainRate`, and an idle swell made by a broad footprint wandering on a slow Lissajous path. Both ease off by `calm` while the visitor is interacting. The shading pass (`water.wgsl`) reads the output texture, builds the normal, refracts the backdrop through it with `refraction.wgsl` (three-tap dispersion), adds a GGX sun glint from `specular.wgsl` and a little relief shading. The Source tab shows all of it; `view` switches to the raw height, velocity or normals.

**Layout and format.** `rgba16float` because it is the one format that is renderable, filterable and (write-only) storage-capable in core WebGPU on every device (docs/support-matrix.md section 6): the render variant, the compute variant and every consumer share it, and there is no read-write storage texture to depend on. Heights are in cells, so a stored slope is a pure tangent whatever the grid size. `r32float` would give more mantissa but is unfilterable without a feature, which would break the one-tap bilinear shading.

**Recommendation.** Ship the render variant. Below 512² the compute variant is several times cheaper per step, but a whole frame of either is well under a millisecond at that size, and the render variant is the one with a WebGL2 port, no compat-mode restriction, and the same result. Use compute when the grid is small and the step count is high (a 256² pond at wave speed 1.5 runs 13 steps per frame at 60 Hz: about 0.7 ms render against 0.1 ms compute), or when the sim shares a frame with other compute work. At 1024² and above render is the faster of the two on this machine (0.064 against 0.075 ms), so compute buys nothing there. A tiled shared-memory kernel that does two steps per dispatch would beat both; it is not built.

## Cost

Measured on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal). Per-step figures come from GPU timestamp queries inside `tests/heightfield.test.ts` (run `HEIGHTFIELD_BENCH=1 npx vitest run tests/heightfield.test.ts -t "cost study"`): the span from the first step to the resolve pass, at 10 and at 210 steps per submit, and the slope between them, which removes the ~1 ms floor of a single pass. Dawn rounds timestamps to about 65 µs, so the 256² compute figure is good to roughly ±0.001 ms. Square grids; the demo's grids are wider than tall (256 x 113 at a 2.3:1 canvas) and cost proportionally less. Indicative, not benchmark-run numbers; the scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty for that reason.

Steps per frame follow from the wave speed: `steps/s = c / (C cell)`, which at the default speed and Courant number is about 130 per second at 256 cells (2 per frame at 60 Hz), 260 at 512 and 520 at 1024. A default 256-cell pond costs the solver about 0.2 ms per frame (render, from the per-step figure and its splat and resolve passes) or 0.03 ms (compute); the shading pass over a 1.0 Mpx canvas (1502 x 662, DPR 2) is a quarter to three quarters of a millisecond by the HUD, whose frame figure read 0.7 ms for the compute variant and 1.4 ms for the render variant with a forced 20 steps per frame. That is inside Tier A on this machine. Not measured: any integrated GPU other than Apple's, any phone, any browser other than the Chromium-based one in the dev pane.

Memory: three `rgba16float` textures (two state, one output) plus a one-byte mask: the HUD reads 4.5 MB in total for the demo including its 1.0 Mpx backdrop, a 256² texture is 0.5 MB, and three 1024² textures are 25 MB.

## Pitfalls

- **A stroke made of `displace` calls is dotted.** The first version cut a drag into chunks and displaced a footprint from the start of each chunk to its end. The ends telescope: the trough dug at the end of one chunk is filled by the next chunk's start in the same frame, so a whole stroke cancels to one dot at each end, per frame. `displace` is for a shape that really moves once per frame (the 3D pool's floating object); a pointer trail is dents.
- **The surface level drifts.** A drop adds volume and a reflecting grid never loses it, so under rain the whole pond rises until half-float precision at that height eats the ripples. The `leak` parameter (0.05 per second) relaxes the mean level back; tests set it to 0 to check energy.
- **Rings need resolution.** A drop narrower than about 3 cells aliases into square ripples, and short waves are slow on this grid (numerical dispersion), so very sharp drops trail a train of fine ripples. The solver clamps footprints to 1.5 cells; the demo's default drop is 0.032 canvas heights, 4 cells at 256.
- **Half-float state.** Heights are stored in cells so a visible ripple is 0.01 to 1, well inside half precision, and the energy of an undamped drop drifts down by about 15% over 250 steps (rounding to half precision acts as a very weak damper). A static height of more than a few hundred cells would lose the ripples; the solver clamps at 512.
- **Resizing clears the water.** The grid is re-created when the canvas aspect changes the cell count, and the new texture is zero. Dragging a window edge therefore wipes the pond every few pixels; the ambient rain fills it back in. A resample pass would keep the waves.
- **One `update` per submitted command buffer.** Impulses and parameters go through a uniform buffer written with `queue.writeBuffer`, so two updates encoded before one submit share the last values.
- **Synthetic input arrives in one frame.** Events dispatched in a loop have the same timestamp, so velocity reads zero; space test events a few milliseconds apart.
- **The screenshot lies about time.** A frame captured 20 ms after a tap shows a small ripple; rings need half a second to read.
- **`timestamp-query` has a floor.** Two passes of the same cost differ by 0.065 ms in the readings; use the slope over many steps, not a single step.

## When to use it

An interactive water surface over an image, a pool, a hero or a full-page background that answers a cursor or a finger and is never dead: Tier A as measured here (the render variant, 256 cells, under a millisecond of GPU time). Drive it from `createHeightfield` if you only want the solver; the ripple demo is the smallest consumer. For a surface nobody touches, the analytic `rain ripples` and noise-normal effects cost less and need no state. For caustics, a 3D pool, DOM obstacles, or scroll and audio excitation the same solver is the input (`texture`, `obstacles`, `addDrop`, `displace`). If the page cannot afford a persistent simulation (reduced motion, low-power mode), show the backdrop with a few static rings.
