## How it works

Water that moves. The solver (`src/gpu/sim/shallow-water.ts`) keeps a **depth** and a **momentum** for every cell of a grid laid over a fixed **terrain**, and integrates the shallow-water equations: mass is conserved, a pressure difference accelerates the water, the ground pushes back with friction. A dam breaks and the surge runs out as a front with a bore on it, splits round a pillar, runs up a beach; a spring on a hillside finds the valley and fills the lake at the bottom; a tide floods and drains rock pools. None of that is possible in the wave-equation heightfield of Epic 5 (`ripples`, `pool-3d`), where a bump only ripples in place, nothing flows, and there is no terrain (comparison below).

**The scheme.** Finite volumes: the state `(h, hu, hv)` of a cell changes by the fluxes through its four faces. Each face flux comes from an **HLL approximate Riemann solver** (Einfeldt wave speeds, Toro's special speeds next to dry ground) applied to the two neighbouring states after **Audusse's hydrostatic reconstruction**: both sides are cut down to the water that sits above the higher of the two beds, and the pressure the reconstruction removes is added back to the cell's own momentum equation. That one step is what makes it behave: a lake at rest over any terrain stays at rest (the pressure and the bed slope cancel exactly, to rounding), a cell next to dry ground sees either a wall or a dam break depending on whether the water is higher than the ground, and the depth cannot go negative while the step obeys the CFL rule. A solid obstacle is simply a bed higher than the water beside it, so there is no mask. The step is one pass: every cell reads its four neighbours and recomputes the four fluxes round it (the two cells either side of a face evaluate the identical function of the identical two states, so the mass flux through it is the same number from both sides and water is only moved, never made or lost). Friction is Manning's law, `g n^2 |u| u / h^(1/3)`, applied implicitly so a thin film comes to rest instead of reversing; it is also what stops a film at 1 mm from running away on a slope.

**Why this scheme and not virtual pipes.** The other realistic GPU choice is the virtual-pipes model (per-cell outflow to the four neighbours driven by the surface-height difference, scaled so a cell never gives away more than it holds): simpler, positive by construction, the usual choice for terrain erosion and games. I built the finite-volume one and did **not** build the pipe model, so what follows is argument, not measurement. In its usual form (Mei et al.) the pipe model has no momentum equation in the physical sense: the stored flux persists, but its wave speed does not depend on the local depth (it is set by the pipe area you choose, not by `sqrt(g h)`), so a surge over a shallow floor travels as fast as one over deep water; a flux that reverses sign loses its momentum at the clamp at zero, which damps oscillations that physics would keep; and it has no bore. What it buys is a cheaper cell (no square roots, no Riemann solver) and a trivial positivity argument. The finite-volume scheme gets the dry-bed front speed to within 0.5% and a wet-bed bore to within 0.6% (below), which is the evidence for choosing it for something sold on "the water rushes out". The flux layout it needs is also smaller than it sounds: no flux textures, one state texture.

**State and formats.** `rgba32float` ping-pong, one texel per cell: `(depth, hu, hv, bed)`. That is **not** the `rgba16float` that docs/support-matrix.md recommends for simulation state, and the reason is mass. A cell's change per step is a small fraction of its depth, below the resolution of a half float (2^-11), so rounding discards it unevenly on the two sides of a face and the volume drifts; and over uneven terrain the surface `bed + depth` is not representable in half precision, so a lake at rest creeps. `rgba32float` is renderable and a write-only storage format in core WebGPU, and the solver only ever reads it with `textureLoad`, so the lack of `float32-filterable` does not matter. The outputs consumers read are two filterable `rgba16float` textures resolved once per update (`src/wgsl/shallow-water.wgsl` decodes them): `water = (depth, velocity x, velocity y, foam)` and `ground = (bed, surface slope x, slope y, ground wetness)`. The surface slope is a Sobel gradient of the water level taken wet-aware (a dry neighbour presents the centre's own level, so the surface is flat up to the shore instead of tilting up the beach), which is also what lets a pixel shader get a normal from one tap.

**Passes and timestep.** Per update: an optional _upload_ pass (terrain or water set by `setTerrain` / `setWater`), N _step_ passes, and one _resolve_ pass that writes `water`, `ground` and a small foam/wetness history in one go. The compute variant puts all N steps in **one compute pass** of N dispatches, which is allowed because each dispatch is its own usage scope (it checks out under `expectValid`); the render variant needs one render pass per step. The timestep is never chosen by the caller: `dt = cfl * dx / (2 * lambda_cap)` with `lambda_cap = 2 sqrt(g * maxDepth)`. The shader enforces the bound it relies on: every step clamps `depth <= maxDepth` and then `|u| + sqrt(g h) <= lambda_cap` in every cell (Ritter's dry dam-break solution has `|u| + c <= 2 sqrt(g h0)`, so the clamp never touches a physical dam break), and `cfl` is clamped to 0.5, the positivity limit of the 2D HLL update. The price is that water deeper than `maxDepth` is discarded, so each scenario sets it a little above its deepest water (1.3 m for a 1 m reservoir), and that a bigger `maxDepth` costs steps (`dt ~ 1 / sqrt(maxDepth)`). `createFixedStepper` feeds the steps, so the picture is the same at 60 and 120 Hz (a test compares them) and a stalled tab runs at most 48 steps and carries on in slow motion, never unstable.

**Sources and interaction.** `addSource` is immediate-mode (call it every frame; it is dropped after one update): a disc that adds or removes water at a stated **volume per second** (the footprint weights are normalised on the CPU against the cells they actually cover, so a source at the grid corner still adds exactly its volume), pushes the water with an acceleration, and raises or digs the bed (the water above rides with it, so the volume is unchanged). `addLevelZone` pulls the water surface in a rectangle toward a level, which is a tide or an inflow boundary. Edges are walls (a mirrored ghost cell) or open (dry ground at the same height, so water runs off like a free outfall).

**The demo.** One scene pass in one module (`shallow.wgsl`): the terrain and the water are two displaced grids with one vertex per cell (no vertex buffer; the vertex stage reads the solver's textures), the diorama sides are procedural strips, and a translucent strip along each edge closes the water like the glass of a tank. The water is an alpha-blended sheet whose coverage is a smooth function of depth, so the shoreline is a contour of the depth field and never steps with the cells. Shading: the bed seen where the refracted ray lands, lit and run through `absorb_composite` (water types from `absorption.wgsl`, with a density multiplier because a 1 m deep pool is clear in real life), Fresnel-weighted sky reflection, a GGX sun glint (`specular.wgsl`), ripples that travel with the flow (two-phase advection of a noise slope field, `flow.wgsl`; calm water stays glassy), and foam. Foam is born in the resolve pass where the flow converges (a bore), on thin supercritical sheets and at advancing fronts, is carried with the flow by a half-float history texture and fades over a second; a faint lace is added at the waterline from `foam.wgsl`. The terrain is sand turning to rock (and meadow in the hillside scene), darkened by the solver's ground wetness, which lags behind a retreating shore, lit with a short shadow march through the height field. The pointer does a ray march against a CPU copy of the bed; the tools are pour, drain, push, raise, lower and orbit (shift inverts), and a tap acts for at least 0.28 s so it leaves something to see. Touch drags scroll the page unless `touch drags` says the water takes them (the same policy as `ripples`; `pool-3d`, a showpiece, always captures). Changing `scenario`, flipping `restart`, changing the grid or the solver restarts the scene; `autoRestart` re-runs the dam break by itself after a pause.

### Comparison with the wave-equation heightfield (Epic 5)

|                                                         | wave-equation heightfield (`ripples`, `pool-3d`)                                                            | shallow-water solver (this)                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What moves                                              | a disturbance in a surface; the water never goes anywhere                                                   | water mass, with a velocity, from one place to another                                                                                                           |
| Equations                                               | `h_tt = c^2 laplacian(h)`; one linear wave speed `c`                                                        | conservation of mass and momentum; the wave speed `sqrt(g h)` depends on the local depth                                                                         |
| Terrain                                                 | none (a flat floor, an obstacle mask that reflects waves)                                                   | an arbitrary height map; water pools in low ground and leaves high ground dry                                                                                    |
| What it can do                                          | ripples, rain rings, a ball pushing a wake, caustics from a smooth surface                                  | dam breaks, floods, rivers, a tide in rock pools, water poured and dug                                                                                           |
| What it cannot do                                       | flow, pour, drain, dry out, break; volume is not even conserved (a `leak` parameter relaxes the mean level) | stay smooth under a poke (a first-order scheme smears a ripple of a few cells), carry thin ripples for long, refract through a clean surface the way a pool does |
| State                                                   | 3 half-float textures (2 state + 1 output), 24 bytes per cell                                               | 2 float32 state + 1 float32 upload + 2 half-float outputs + 2 half-float history, 80 bytes per cell                                                              |
| Cost per step, 256 cells on the long side, this machine | 0.007 ms (compute) / 0.054 ms (render), Epic 5's figures                                                    | 0.009 to 0.017 ms (compute, 256 x 171) / 0.051 ms (render): the HLL step is heavier per cell, the render-pass floor is the same                                  |
| Steps per frame at 60 Hz                                | 2 to 13 (set by the wave speed)                                                                             | about 10 at 256 cells, 15 at 384 (set by the deepest water the scene may hold and the cell size)                                                                 |
| Stability                                               | `c dt / dx <= 0.866`, clamped                                                                               | `dt = cfl dx / (2 lambda_cap)` with `lambda_cap = 2 sqrt(g maxDepth)`, enforced by clamps in the shader                                                          |
| When a website wants it                                 | an interactive surface that is never dead, over an image or a pool: Tier A                                  | water that has to go somewhere: floods, rivers, dams, rain on terrain, a game toy: Tier B                                                                        |

They are not rivals: the heightfield is the right way to draw fine ripples on a calm pool, and the shallow-water solver is the right way to make the pool fill. A real page that wanted both would run the shallow-water solver for the bulk flow and add ripple normals (as the demo does with noise) on top.

## Cost

Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal), Chromium in the browser pane, GPU shared with other work, so everything here is **indicative**; the scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty for that reason. A trivial pass reads about 1.2 ms here, so single-pass readings near that mean "too cheap to measure".

_Solver, per step_ (`SHALLOW_WATER_BENCH=1 npx vitest run tests/shallow-water.test.ts -t "cost study"`: timestamp queries from the first pass to the last, at 10 and at 210 steps, the slope between them removes the floor; quiet-machine runs, the first of three was disturbed and is left out). Grids are 3:2; the demo's default is 256 x 171.

| Grid       | compute, ms per step | render, ms per step |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| 128 x 85   | 0.006 to 0.008       | 0.050 to 0.055      |
| 256 x 171  | 0.009 to 0.013       | 0.051               |
| 512 x 341  | 0.024 to 0.030       | 0.051 to 0.055      |
| 1024 x 683 | 0.084 to 0.100       | 0.089 to 0.109      |

The same picture as Epic 5: a render pass has a fixed cost of about 0.05 ms and almost no per-cell cost below 512 cells, a compute dispatch scales with the cells, and the two meet near a million cells. What differs is the step count. A wave equation needs 2 to 13 steps a frame; this scheme's step is set by `sqrt(g * maxDepth)` and the cell size, about 10 per frame at 256 cells and 15 at 384 for a 1.3 m scene at 60 Hz and the default time scale. At 10 steps the render variant costs 0.5 ms a frame and the compute variant 0.1 to 0.15 ms, **so compute is the default**, and that is the choice that makes `scorecard.compute` true. The render variant is kept (`solver: render`): same maths in the same WGSL core, the same results to within rounding (a test compares them), no storage textures, and the one that could be ported to WebGL2. In the browser pane the `sim steps` pass read 2.5 ms for 150 steps (0.017 ms a step) at 256 x 171.

_Whole frame_ at the default scene, canvas 1702 x 787 (1.34 Mpx, DPR 2), 120 fps display, defaults: frame GPU span **3.0 to 3.3 ms**, of which the scene pass is 2.6 to 3.0 ms, the solver's steps 0.1 to 0.2 ms (5 steps a frame at 120 Hz) and the resolve pass 0.03 ms. Repeating the scene draw three times (the repeat-N method) moved the pass from 3.6 to 7.9 ms with the lake covering the basin and from 2.6 to 3.6 ms in the dry early state: the marginal cost of a scene draw is about 0.5 ms with mostly sand and about 2.5 ms with the whole grid under water. **The water fragment shader dominates**, by 15 to 25 times over the solver: a shadow march (9 taps), the bed lookup (a refracted offset, 5 taps, an albedo), ripples (four 3D noise derivatives), the foam texture (two Voronoi cells and a noise) and the sky and glint. The terrain pass skips shading under water more than 6 cm deep (it would be covered anyway). The 6 ms Tier B budget therefore has room on this machine; on an integrated GPU the fragment cost scales with pixels, which is what the HUD's resolution scale is for. Not measured: any GPU other than Apple's, any phone, any browser other than Chromium.

_Memory._ 80 bytes a cell. At 256 x 171 (43,776 cells): two float32 state textures 1.4 MB, an upload texture 0.7 MB, two output and two history textures 0.35 MB each, 3.5 MB in all; the HUD read 8.4 MB for the whole effect including the depth buffer. At 1024 x 683 the solver is 56 MB.

_Accuracy and conservation_ (tests/shallow-water.test.ts, both variants identical). Dry dam break against Ritter's analytic solution at t = 0.6 s, h0 = 1 m: the depth profile is within **1.4% (L1)** of the exact one on 192 cells and 0.9% on 384; the front where the depth falls below 5% of h0 is within 0.0% / 1.3% of the exact position and where it falls below 1% is 10% / 8% short (a first-order scheme rounds the thin tip off), and nothing travels faster than the exact tip `2 sqrt(g h0)`. A wet-bed bore (h1 = 0.2 h0) against Stoker's solution: bore position within **0.55%**, plateau depth within 0.23%. A lake at rest over rolling hills and pillars, with dry hilltops standing out, for 8 simulated seconds: 1.5e-6 m/s residual speed, 6e-8 m level error. Volume in a closed basin through a dam break with wetting and drying: relative drift **2e-7 over 8,100 steps**. Depth never negative, never NaN or Inf, and `|u| + sqrt(g h) <= lambda_cap` in every cell in a violent dam break over pillars.

## Pitfalls

- **Half-float state cannot conserve mass.** Not tried: ruled out by arithmetic (see state and formats), so the claim is an argument, not a measurement. It is why the state is `rgba32float` against the project's usual advice. The outputs are half floats and are fine for shading, not for bookkeeping; tests read the exact `stateTexture`.
- **`maxDepth` is part of the stability rule, not a nicety.** The timestep comes from it and water deeper than it is discarded. A scene that pours into a closed basin eventually reaches it and then silently loses water; the demo's scenes stay below theirs. Raising it is always safe and costs steps.
- **A thin film does not run away, and that is the point of Manning friction.** A sheet of a few millimetres on a slope holds up for many seconds (after 60 simulated seconds 95% of the spring's water in the test is in the pool and the rest is film on the slope). Without friction the film would accelerate until the velocity clamp stopped it, and the screen would flicker.
- **First order smears.** A fine ripple (a few cells) is flattened within a fraction of a second, and the thin tip of a dry-bed front is 8 to 10% behind the exact contour. This is a flood simulator, not a ripple tank; for fine ripples use the Epic 5 heightfield or the noise ripples the demo adds.
- **Bed and depth are separate: set the terrain before the water.** `setTerrain` replaces the bed and leaves the water; `setWater` replaces the water (at rest) and leaves the bed. After `setResolution` everything is empty again and the scene has to be supplied again; the demo's restart does exactly that.
- **One uniform write per update.** The sources and the upload mode go in the same uniform buffer as everything else, written with `queue.writeBuffer`, which lands before the whole command buffer: two updates encoded into one submit share the last values. (A first draft wrote the uniforms twice in one update and the upload pass saw the step pass's values.)
- **A new solver starts counting generations again.** The first version of the demo keyed its bind group on `generation` alone, so switching the solver variant reused a bind group whose textures had just been destroyed. The headless effect test caught it; the key is now the solver and its generation.
- **Reserved words in WGSL.** `patch` is one, and a single shader error blanks the whole effect; the source overlay names the line.
- **Picking hits the ground, not the water.** The ray is marched against a CPU copy of the bed, so over a 1 m deep reservoir the brush lands up to 0.7 m off from where the ray crosses the surface. A GPU pick against `bed + depth` would fix it; not built.
- **Foam is a texture of the flow, not a particle system.** It follows the velocity field and fades, which reads well on bores and rapids, but a lump of foam left behind a pillar cannot be stirred apart or sink.
- **The synthetic-input trap.** The pointer tracker needs events a few milliseconds apart to read a drag; the demo's tap time (0.28 s) is what lets a single synthetic press show anything.
- **The first screenshot after a navigation is blank, and a tab in the background renders nothing.** Front the tab, wait for the page, then measure.

## When to use it

Tier B, compute: a feature page where the water has to go somewhere. The dam-break and flood scenes are the obvious fit (a utility, a flood-risk explainer, a game or toy about rivers and dams), and the pour / dig / push tools turn it into something to play with. It holds a 6 ms budget on Apple Silicon with room to spare (about 3 ms a frame at 1.3 Mpx, the solver a tenth of that); the shading is what to trim on weaker GPUs (fewer shadow taps, no ripples, the resolution scale), not the simulation, which at 256 cells is cheap enough for a phone to try. It needs compute for its default path (`solver: render` is the same maths without it and costs about 0.5 ms a frame instead of 0.1). Mobile is untested. Where WebGPU is missing, show a looping video of the dam break; there is no CSS fallback worth the name.

Reach for something else when the water should look like a calm pool or a ripple (the Epic 5 heightfield: Tier A, a tenth of the cost), when the surface must stay smooth under a poke, when the liquid has to splash and break (Epic 7, particles), or when the scene is an open ocean (Epic 8: nothing in it has to run downhill).
