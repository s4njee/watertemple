## How it works

A wind tunnel on the stable-fluids solver (`src/gpu/sim/fluid.ts`; its grid, schemes and costs are in the `fluid-dye` notes). A steady inflow blows from the left, rows of dye are fed in at the inlet so the flow is visible, and solid shapes stand in the stream. The flow goes **round** them because the solver treats the obstacle mask as solid: faces next to a solid cell are held at velocity zero in advection, divergence and projection, solid neighbours are left out of the interpolation (so the fluid slides along the surface instead of being dragged to a stop), and the dye in a solid cell is zeroed every step. This effect adds three things to that: how shapes get into the mask, how a wind is blown in, and how a shape that moves pushes the fluid.

**Shapes: `src/gpu/sim/obstacle-shapes.ts`.** A circle, a box, a rounded box, a capsule and a wing (a teardrop: the convex hull of a round leading edge and a tiny circle at the tail, the "uneven capsule" distance, built from `sd_circle`, `sd_box_2d`, `sd_rounded_box_2d` and `sd_capsule_2d` of `sdf.wgsl` plus that one primitive) with a centre, a rotation (positive is clockwise on screen) and a size. A table of up to 64 shapes (three `vec4f` each) goes to a uniform buffer; `obstacle-raster.wgsl` draws it into `fluid.obstacles.texture` with a render pass (one `r8unorm` texel per solver cell, 1 where the **cell centre** is inside a shape), only when something changed or the solver re-created its grids. The same table and `obstacle-shapes.wgsl` are what `display.wgsl` includes to draw the shapes crisp: anti-aliased from the signed distance, with a bevel from the distance gradient, not from the mask. A CPU copy of the distance (`obstacleDistance`) serves picking (a press grabs the topmost shape within a few hundredths of a height) and the tests, which compare the GPU mask to it cell for cell.

**Page elements.** `trackDomRects` exactly as `ripple-dom` uses it: elements marked `data-wt-obstacle` (the page contexts already carry the markup) are measured every frame, turned into rounded boxes (`shapeFromRect`, with the velocity of their centre from the edge motion) and appended to the table. `Scene: auto` (the default) takes the page's own elements if the page has any and the cylinder otherwise, so `&layout=hero|cards|backdrop` streams the dye round the real cards and headline with no setting; `page elements` in the bare layout adds the three demonstration cards of `ripple-dom` (read-only import of its `demo.ts`), one of them draggable with the pointer or the arrow keys. Page shapes are drawn as plain ground in the canvas (the real element is on top).

**The wind: a force band, not a boundary condition.** The solver's `open` edge makes the cells outside air at pressure zero, which lets the flow leave and clear water enter but cannot carry a prescribed velocity, so a steady inflow is imposed as a body force: in `obstacle-drive.wgsl`, a pass for the solver's `forces` hook (after the built-in forces, before the projection, once per step), velocities in a band at the left edge are blended toward the wind by 0.45 per step. The projection then makes the whole field consistent with the shapes downstream, so the band is a soft source rather than a wall. The top and bottom edges are `open` too (a tunnel of free air, not a duct), so a big body diverts flow out of the sides. The wind eases in over a second. The dye is a second hook (`inlet.wgsl`, the `dye` hook): after the dye is advected, a band at the inlet **replaces** it with a pattern (14 thin rows alternating warm and cool, fewer thick bands, a full-height "timeline" every 0.45 s, or none), which makes streaklines of any length.

**What a moving shape does to the fluid.** Not what the solver does by itself. A solid in the solver is static: a shape that moves only changes the mask, the fluid and dye in the cells it lands on are deleted (the tests show nothing is ever left inside a shape), and the cells it leaves are born clear and at rest. It pushes nothing: the fluid ahead of it just vanishes. The same `forces` pass therefore supplies a moving boundary: in a band of 3 cells round each shape the fluid takes the shape's velocity (its translation plus spin, `shape.velocity` and `shape.spin`) with weight falling to zero at the edge of the band, and the projection that follows turns the mismatch into pressure. Two modes: relaxing only the **normal** component to the solid's own (free slip) turned out to push little (the projection takes most of a normal-only velocity back out). Measured in a closed box as the mean fluid velocity just ahead of a cylinder dragged at 0.8 image heights per second: 0.013 for a normal-only 2-cell band, 0.071 for a normal-only 4-cell band, 0.096 for a whole-velocity 2-cell band and 0.185 for a whole-velocity 4-cell band (0.210 at 6 cells), against 0 with the drive off. So a moving shape drags the whole velocity in its band (no slip), and a shape at rest gets free slip only (the pegs of the tunnel must not be sticky). The result: the fluid ahead of a dragged shape is shoved along with it, the fluid behind is drawn in after it, a spinning bar sets the fluid turning the same way, and dragging a shape through uniformly dyed fluid leaves no dye in its new footprint (test), at any speed, with every velocity inside the solver's own cap. What is not done is a true moving-boundary condition inside the pressure solve (the divergence and the projection would have to read the solid's velocity at blocked faces), which is a change to the solver; this is the stable approximation available outside it. The effect also limits the drag: a grabbed shape follows the pointer at no more than 1.6 heights per second, so it does not teleport.

**The vortex street.** The default preset is a cylinder of diameter 0.2 canvas heights in a wind of 0.9 heights per second with viscosity 2e-4 (nominal Reynolds number U D / nu = 900; the solver's numerical diffusion makes the effective one lower), vorticity confinement 3, a 144-cell short side (256 x 144 cells at 16:9), 50 Jacobi iterations, MacCormack for velocity and dye. A symmetric body needs something to break the symmetry, so on every scene start a small off-axis push is added downstream of the first shape. From rest the wake rolls up within about 9 s and by 15 to 20 s a clear alternating street has formed (vorticity of opposite sign in a staggered double row, the `vortex street (vorticity view)` preset; the dye view shows it as streaklines rolling up). Seen in offscreen renders at 880 x 495 at 144 cells, and in the browser pane; the other resolutions were not compared in the picture, and the Strouhal number and the Reynolds number were not measured against the textbook values (St about 0.2): the street is established qualitatively only.

## Cost

Measured on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal through Dawn), 1920 x 1080 target, **indicative**: another process shared the GPU, so the median frame was two to three times the best one (the scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty). GPU timestamps over whole frames (a scratch test, not committed): the **best of 50 frames** was 4.8 ms at 96 cells, 5.0 ms at the default 144, 7.0 ms at 192 (dye 2 texels per cell, 50 pressure iterations); 100 iterations or the other scenes moved it by less than the noise. A 256-cell run (with the earlier, finer dye of three texels per cell) took about 39 ms a frame by wall-clock submit throughput while the GPU was busy, so 256 is a showpiece setting only. In the browser pane, canvas 502 x 592 (cards layout, DPR 2), the HUD read 40 fps and 8.9 ms of GPU for the frame with the pane busy, against the same 6 ms Tier B budget: over on this machine under contention, within it on the best frames. The per-pass numbers of both are polluted by queue waits and are not quoted; the HUD's `dye inlet` and `shade` rows at 5 to 12 ms are the passes that waited.

What dominates, by construction rather than by a clean profile: dependent dispatches. A step is about 60 of them (two for MacCormack on each field, up to 50 Jacobi iterations, curl, forces, project, resolve), plus the mask taps that every interpolation pays once solids are enabled (each face tap asks the mask for two cells), plus the drive and inlet passes and the draw. The resolution barely moves the best frame from 96 to 144 because the dispatch count, not the cell count, is the floor; above that the cell count takes over. Memory: velocity 3 textures of (W+1) x (H+1) x 8 B (0.9 MB at 256 x 144), dye 3 of 512 x 288 x 8 B (3.5 MB), pressure and the rest about 1.5 MB, the mask 37 kB, the shape table 3 kB. Tier B on this machine, borderline: on a weaker GPU use 96 cells and 24 iterations, or fall back to the video.

## Pitfalls

- **A solid is a staircase.** The mask is the cells whose centre is inside a shape, so the true outline and the solver's differ by up to half a cell (1.7 percent of the diameter of the 29-cell cylinder, at most), the flow sees steps, and the dye stops at the steps. Drawing the exact SDF outline grown by 0.45 cell over it covers the difference: without the growth a ring of dye-free water showed between the outline and the cells. The `mask` view draws the solver's cells in red and the exact outline in green to show it.
- **A thin shape can fall between cell centres.** A plate thinner than a cell may vanish or break into pieces; the plate scene is two cells thick and the test of a one-cell wall places it exactly on a column of cell centres. `setGrow` counts cells within a distance of the shape. A one-cell wall is leak-free at corners and across dye: a closed box of one-cell walls with dye shoved at every wall and corner lets nothing out (test).
- **A diagonal wall needs two cells.** Two diagonal solid cells touch only at a corner, and the dye's bilinear taps at that point mix both sides; a diagonal bar two cells thick (face-connected) holds dye completely (test), a one-cell diagonal would leak a little. The solver's, not fixed here.
- **Moving solids are an approximation** (above): the push is real but not exact, and a shape that jumps many cells in one frame deletes the fluid it jumps over instead of pushing it. The effect rate-limits the drag; the tests jump a shape 0.7 of the canvas in one frame and get a finite, bounded field with nothing inside it.
- **Open edges and the wind band are not a true inflow.** The band blends velocity, it does not fix it: with a large body the wind in the band is a little lower than the target and the dye rows near the inlet bend before the body. The inflow is soft by 0.07 heights; the vortex street shed by a body close to the inlet feeds back into it.
- **Dye is not conserved** (see the `fluid-dye` notes): it is a tracer advected by MacCormack, and the inlet replaces rather than adds, so there is no accumulation. Dye never appears inside a shape or behind a one-cell wall, and nothing sticks in a halo (the solver zeroes dye in solid cells and its interpolation ignores them).
- **Vorticity confinement amplifies cell noise.** The vorticity view shows a faint chequerboard in quiet flow at the gain of the preset; it is invisible in the dye view.
- **One slot per hook.** `shapes.attach()` takes `fluid.hooks.forces` and the effect takes `hooks.dye`; a second consumer of either must chain them itself. `detach` puts back what was there.
- **A hidden browser pane does not run the page**; the street only develops while the pane is visible.

## When to use it

Where a flow should visibly respect the layout: ink, smoke or colour streaming round the real cards and headline of a hero (`&layout=hero`), a product explainer that shows flow past a wing or a pipe, a playful page where visitors drag objects through a current. It needs compute shaders (no WebGL2 port without rewriting the solver as render passes) and costs a solver step per frame, Tier B. **Page elements are rectangles only**: the box of each element (the largest of its four corner radii, a transformed element is its bounding box, text is the box of its element), measured every frame through `getBoundingClientRect`; an element with a complex outline (a rotated card, an SVG shape, a wrapped paragraph) is its box, and the solver sees it at the grid's resolution. Dye through a 14-row inlet is a visualisation; for a real site, pick one of the thin patterns and a gentle wind (0.3 to 0.6) so it reads as ambient, not a wind tunnel. Use the cheap animated `ink-wash` background where nothing needs to touch anything, and a looping video where the flow does not need to react to the page.
