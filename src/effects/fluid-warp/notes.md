## How it works

The picture is not shown by the fluid, it is pushed by it. `src/gpu/sim/fluid-warp.ts` keeps a **displacement field** D next to the stable-fluids solver (`fluid.ts`, see the `fluid-dye` notes for the solver itself): one `rgba16float` texel per sample stores how far the content at that point has been moved from where it rests, in image heights (y down), the unit of the solver's velocity. Every solver step the field is advected by the velocity, gains the motion the flow just gave it, relaxes toward zero and is clamped (`fluid-warp-advect.wgsl`, a fullscreen fragment pass):

```
v1  = v(x - dt/2 v(x))                    velocity at the middle of the parcel's path
D'  = D(x - dt v1) + gain dt v1           what the parcel carried, plus what it just moved
D' *= exp(-relaxation dt)                 spring back (0 = a smear is permanent, like paint)
|D'| <= maxDisplacement                   the content cannot be dragged off to infinity
```

That is the discretisation of `dD/dt + (v . grad) D = v - r D`. A parcel of fluid carries the content on it, and the content at the parcel is displaced by exactly the distance the parcel has travelled from rest, so along its path `dD/dt = v`; written for a fixed point it is advection plus a source. A steady uniform flow for a time t gives D = v t exactly, and a solid-body rotation gives |D| = 2 r sin(theta / 2): both are tests in `tests/fluid-warp.test.ts`, along with the decay `exp(-r t)` of the relaxation, the clamp under violent stirring, and a field that stays finite.

**Drawing is a backward map.** `fluid-warp-apply.wgsl` samples the content at `uv - D` (`fwarp_source_uv` in `fluid-warp-core.wgsl`): every output pixel asks where its colour comes from. A forward map (push each source texel to `uv + D`) leaves holes where the flow stretches the content and piles texels up where it compresses and would need splatting; the backward map has neither, and D = 0 returns the source unchanged. The direction matters and is tested: content moves WITH the flow, so a marker in a uniform flow of `(vx, vy)` ends up `v t` further along, not behind. With D exactly zero the shader takes none of its other branches, and the output is the source to the byte (the test renders random noise and compares every channel within one level, with chroma and sheen switched on).

**Where the field lives.** Three options were weighed. (1) The solver's own dye texture, driven through `fluid.hooks.dye`: no extra target, the solver's MacCormack advection and boundaries for free, and dye dissipation as the relaxation. Rejected because it takes the dye away from the fluid (an effect could not show ink and warp content together), ties the relaxation to the dye's exponential decay and clamp (`dyeLimit` is at least 1 image height, far more than a sensible displacement), and fixes the field to the dye's grid. (2) A field of its own, stepped from `hooks.dye` as a per-step callback so it sees every solver step: what is built. It costs one `rgba16float` texture pair (about two texels per solver cell each way: 342 x 192 at the default) and one pass per step, and is independent of whatever the solver does with its dye. (3) Re-integrating the field from the final velocity once per frame: simpler, but the solver may run up to four steps a frame and the field would see one, so the content would lag the flow at low frame rates. The field is advected semi-Lagrangian rather than MacCormack: it is smooth, so the numerical diffusion that blurs dye only softens the smear a little, and it cannot overshoot (a corrected scheme on a field that is allowed to go negative would need its own limiter for no visible gain). Sampling uses a cubic B-spline in four bilinear taps (`fwarp_fetch_cubic`) so the displacement is C2 smooth and a coarse field shows no creases.

**The look.** `chroma` splits the channels along the displacement in proportion to the local stretch (the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian of D from four more taps): in a pure translation they do not part, where the content is smeared they do. `sheen` treats |D| as the thickness of a liquid film and shades the content by its gradient (a soft light and a small glint), both exactly zero at rest. The border is handled by `refraction_fade_offset` and a mirror fold, so a displacement that points at an edge fades out before it and the border pixel is never smeared or wrapped; with the `wall` boundary the flow itself cannot leave, so the fold is a guard. Debug views: the displacement (hue is direction), the deformed grid (the content's own coordinates drawn as lines, which shows the fold of the map), and the solver's velocity.

**Content.** An image (the harbour still painted on a 2D canvas, read-only reuse of `media-distortion/sources.ts`), a live video (`captureStream`, same source), a dropped file (`createMediaFromFile`), and a **headline** drawn into a 2D canvas at device resolution (`headline.ts`: the font and three strings are constants; the type is sized to fill the canvas, cut into one to three lines, with ruled lines and dots in the empty parts so the flow shows where there is no type), uploaded without mips so it is 1:1 with the pixels, and redrawn once the canvas has stopped resizing for 0.2 s. Without a DOM (the tests) the shared test card stands in for every source.

**Interaction.** `createFluidStirrer` turns pointers into capsule splats of momentum (no dye). The pointer policy is the one of `fluid-dye`: a press-drag stirs, a hover pushes at a third of the strength, touch drags scroll the page unless `Touch drags` is `stirs the content` (then the canvas takes the pointer, `touch-action: none`). An idle stir wanders on a Lissajous path so the content breathes by a few percent of its height; it eases off while someone is touching it and scales with the spring back, so it is off for a smear that never relaxes (an idle hand must not wreck a painting).

## Cost

Measured on the Apple Silicon development laptop (Metal through Dawn), a 1920 x 1080 target, with other agents sharing the GPU, so **indicative**; the scorecard leaves `gpuMs` empty. GPU timestamps over whole frames (first timed pass to last, `tests` scratch, not committed): the **best of 50 frames** was 1.4 ms with 64 fluid cells on the short side, 1.7 ms at the default 96, 2.5 ms at 128 and 4.4 ms at 192; the median was two to four times that while another process held the GPU, so quote the minimum. The per-pass figures of that run were not usable (queue waits inside the shared GPU). In the browser pane (canvas 1662 x 932, DPR 2) the HUD read 120 fps with the shade pass at 0.26 ms (the 1 ms floor of the HUD, "too cheap to measure").

What dominates: the solver, by construction rather than by a clean profile (the per-pass timestamps of the shared GPU were not usable). A step is dozens of dependent dispatches (MacCormack advection, 24 Jacobi iterations, forces, projection), while the field pass touches a 342 x 192 texture and the final draw is 4 + 4 + 3 taps per pixel. Raising the fluid resolution from 96 to 192 more than doubled the best frame; the field's own resolution (`scale`, default 2 texels per cell) was not varied. The dye the solver carries is unused and kept at its smallest grid (16 cells) with the cheapest scheme. Memory: the field pair 2 x 0.5 MB at the default, the solver about 2 MB, the content 4.9 MB (the harbour with mips) or 6.2 MB (a 1662 x 932 headline, no mips). Tier B on this machine; not measured on an integrated Intel or Mali GPU or on a phone.

Against **`media-distortion`** (Epic 4, one pass, three taps of a baked normal map, about 1 ms at the HUD floor): the simulation is two to three times the GPU cost (about 1.7 to 2.5 ms at best, a solver step plus a draw) and a few hundred lines more. What it buys is **memory and physics**: the water effect only wobbles, a function of position and time, so it cannot remember where the pointer was, cannot smear something and leave it smeared, cannot make a swirl that turns on itself or a shear between two parts of the picture, and has no notion of how long a disturbance lasts. Here the same stroke leaves a persistent fold, the flow it makes is incompressible (so it circulates round the stroke), a wet paint and a jelly are one parameter apart, and a headline is dragged along and reassembles at a chosen rate.

## Pitfalls

- **Direction and the backward map.** The content has to be sampled at `uv - D`, not `uv + D`, and D must accumulate `+v dt`: the opposite sign pair makes the content slide against the flow, and a forward map tears. The tests drive a known flow and read where a marker lands.
- **A smear is only as sharp as its field.** Bilinear semi-Lagrangian advection of D smooths it; a long stroke of a permanent smear slowly softens. With the cubic filter the fold of a heavy smear is smooth rather than creased, which is also why the debug grid view shows soft curves.
- **Half-float accumulation.** D is `rgba16float`: three decimal digits. A step adds `v dt`, about 0.008 image heights at a speed of 0.5, far above the half-float spacing of a displacement of a few tenths (2.4e-4 at 0.3), but a flow slower than about 0.02 heights per second stops adding once |D| is above 0.3. Far below a pixel.
- **The same `dt` in a frame.** The field step reads one uniform buffer; every step of a submitted command buffer must use the same step length (the solver's fixed step guarantees it).
- **The fluid owns the dye hook.** `warp.attach()` takes `fluid.hooks.dye` (and chains a hook that was there); an effect that wants dye as well must not replace it.
- **The solver's dye is wasted work.** The solver advects a dye grid whether or not anyone reads it; the effect keeps it at 16 cells and semi-Lagrangian, which costs next to nothing, but a solver option to skip the pass would be cleaner.
- **Resizing clears the smear.** When the canvas changes shape by more than 6 percent the solver's grids are re-created (the field follows and starts at rest); smaller changes stretch the picture a few percent instead.
- **The headline is drawn per size.** It is rendered at device resolution, so a resize needs a redraw (debounced); a stale texture is stretched over the new size for a moment. A webfont that has not loaded when the card is drawn falls back to the next in the stack.
- **A hidden pane does not run the page.** In the development browser pane a hidden tab is not composited; the effect only advances while it is visible.

## When to use it

A hero image that smears like wet paint under the cursor and settles back, a playful headline whose letters are dragged along and reassemble, a hover or page-transition smear over an image or a looping video. Pick the spring back first: 0 is paint (permanent, a one-shot art piece), 1 a wet sheet that clears in a few seconds, 4 and above jelly. It is Tier B: a solver step and two small passes, a few times the cost of a Tier A distortion. Use `media-distortion` instead when the picture only needs to shimmer or answer a hover and nobody will drag it, and CSS (a hover transform per word, or an SVG `feDisplacementMap`) when the content must stay live HTML.

**The honest limit: it warps a texture.** The canvas can only distort pixels it has, so live DOM content has to be turned into a texture first. The text here is drawn into a 2D canvas, a second copy of the headline that is not selectable, not translatable, not read by a screen reader, and that does not restyle with the page (keep the real heading in the DOM, hide it visually while the canvas is up, or give the canvas `aria-hidden` and keep the real one as the accessible text). Getting arbitrary HTML into a texture is the open problem of story 9.1: `docs/support-matrix.md` section 5.2 records that HTML-in-Canvas is a Chrome-only origin trial with two incompatible sets of names, so images, video and 2D-canvas rendering are the baseline and the HTML path is a progressive enhancement. Where WebGPU or compute is missing show the plain image, video or headline, optionally with a CSS filter on hover.
